Foreword

As a component of its Biodiversity Conservation
and Resource Management Programme
(BCRMP), the United Nations Development
Programme assisted the Government of Papua
New Guinea in its effort to establish a conservation
area in the southern part of New Ireland Province.
The objective of the Lak Integrated Conservation
and Development (ICAD) project was to address
development needs of the population in a
sustainable way to conserve the rich biodiversity in
the area.

Because the Lak project was an ICAD project,
it focused on development initiatives such as the
design of a sustainable forestry project. However,
because a commercial logging operation was
already under way, the resource base for the
sustainable forestry project diminished rapidly.
Hence the project turned out to be unviable and
did not result in the establishment of a
conservation area.

Why, therefore, is the Lak ICAD project
considered as having been successful? The answer
is simple: the programme was expressly mandated
to experiment, design and trial innovative methods
for gaining customary landowner support for
conservation initiatives. In the case of Lak, the
experimental result may have been negative, but
the outcome has been positive.

The principal positive outcome of the Lak
project is that invaluable lessons have been
learned. Chiefly contributing to this outcome is
the comprehensive way in which the experiment
itself was documented. This book, with which the
United Nations Development Programme in
Papua New Guinea is proud to be associated, is
only one of the many publications born out of the
Lak experience. These publications and reports are
being used as tools by many different government

iii

and non-government agencies in Papua New
Guinea actively engaged in sustainable
development initiatives and efforts to secure
biodiversity conservation.

The lessons learned from Lak have also
resulted in the development of the pace-setting
participatory methodology currently being trialled
in the Bismarck—-Ramu ICAD initiative, which is
also part of the BCRMP. As with Lak, the
Bismarck—Ramu ICAD design and
implementation are being thoroughly
documented.

The Global Environment Facility (GEF),
which is jointly managed by the World Bank, the
United Nations Development Programme and the
United Nations Environment Programme and
which provides funding for the BCRMP,
specifically aims to target the intersection of
national and global interests in biodiversity
conservation. Papua New Guinea, one of the most
biodiverse places on the planet, is of
unquestionable global significance. However, the
complexity of the landownership, with 97 per cent
of the country under customary land tenure, limits
the possibilities of the State to preserve this global
interest. Within Papua New Guinea, this
biodiversity is the local heritage and the major
capital resource of the customary landowners
themselves. As the Lak experience demonstrates,
it is the key to their development aspirations.

The challenge is to see that such development
is sustainable, and that biodiversity priorities
are protected.

1.1 Introduction

This document reports on the “lessons
learned” in implementing a conservation initiative
in Lak, New Ireland Province, Papua New Guinea.
The problems faced by the Lak ICAD project are
demonstrative of the obstacles that confront
conservation managers in PNG. The country is

h fast becoming a conservation “hot spot” as its

biodiversity endowment faces accelerating
pressures from logging, agriculture, subsistence
activities and infrastructural development. Lacking
a protected area system that is representative of its
diverse habitats, PNG urgently requires specific
conservation interventions. The search for a
workable conservation instrument is being
frustrated, however, by a range of social, economic
and political factors.

Local communities comprise the principal
stakeholders in PNG’s conservation and forest
management arena. Some 97 per cent of the land
lies under customary tenure, and rights-holders in
communities dictate use of, and control access to,
the forest resource. Conservation managers must
negotiate a compact with these rights-holders in
order to obtain a conservation interest in forests.

» However, PNG’s forests are also valuable timber

reserves, and multinational timber conglomerates,
among other interests, are competing for the
resource. This “competition” is especially acute in
lowland tropical rainforest, a highly bio-diverse
environment. For now, logging concerns are
driving land use allocation at the expense of
conservation.

This situation is complicated by the weak
efficacy of the State in regulating land use on
customary land. With some 8000 traditionally
autonomous tribal groupings, Papua New
Guineans generally have a weak sense of national

identity and concomitant social responsibility
beyond that demanded by custom. Without a
means of regulating private land use in the larger
national and global interest, land use is being
decided based on the private cost-benefit
equations of forest owners, regardless of the
external costs involved.

» The combination of social, economic and
political factors that make up the landscape for
conservation poses considerable challenges for
conservation workers wanting to protect
biodiversity. The problem that conservation faces
is amplified by a combination of stakeholder
interests that result in a push for logging and other
extractive uses of forest resources. The State faces a
fiscal problem, which it is attempting to remedy
through expanding the revenue-yielding forest
industry; politicians face severe demands from
constituents for “fast track” development;
landowners have a time preference for current
income, giving the future little attention; and risk
and uncertainty mitigate against the logging
industry taking a long-term view of management.

2.0 Many conservationists in PNG do not object

<7 to logging intrinsically, recognising that the

industry provides both substantial income and
foreign exchange at a national level, and vital jobs
and income to local communities. However,
logging practices currently being used in PNG are
especially damaging to ecological processes. Not
only are these methods threatening biodiversity,
they are having the effect of preventing future
productive uses of the forest environment. PNG's
forests could potentially provide a number of
sustainable income-earning opportunities. (Not all
of these can be harnessed today owing to
technological constraints and market bottlenecks.)
Opportunities include recreation, the harvest of
eco-timber, exudates, essential oils, rattans,

Race for the Rainforest

mushrooms, nuts and ornamental plants. The
economic viability of these use opportunities,
in the long run, is based on environmental
well-being.

Papua New Guinea’ forest biodiversity, by
providing a range of ecological goods and services,
also underpins the nation’s subsistence economy
(see Sekhran & Miller eds 1994). Of PNG’s rural
population, subsistence provides a safety net for
some, and the principal means of sustenance for
others. Earnings from the formal sector are
insufficient to provide for sustenance, and many
food and other survival requirements are met from
subsistence activities. Biodiversity thus provides
critical welfare support. The loss of these services,
without the corresponding development of
alternative livelihood options, couldleadtoa = #
downward spiral of impoverishment. There is thus
a sound economic reason for wise use of forest
resources. However, as many of the benefits of
forests are non-monetary, they are not included in
|. decision making at the local and national levels.
£ Conservation practitioners are facing the
ehalleage by attempting to forge linkages between
the development goals of customary landowners
and conservation objectives. Through providing
support for community-led development, and
building linkages between such support and
biodiversity conservation, a new conservation
model is being tested. A number of conservation
proponents are piloting Integrated Conservation
and Development Projects (ICADPs) in PNG asa
vehicle for this. The Lak initiative was one such
project. It was developed under the umbrella of
the PNG Biodiversity Conservation and Resource
Management Programme, a joint initiative of the
PNG Department of Environment and
Conservation and the United Nations
Development Programme (details are provided in
Section 1.3).

The Lak ICAD project was the first to directly
confront an existing logging operation, in
attempting to improve forest management and
pursue conservation goals. For a number of
reasons, the project was unsuccessful in achieving
its primary objective. A combination of social,
economic and political factors compromised the
project’s operational environment, reducing the
room to manoeuvre. The existence of a “live”
logging concession in the project area increased
the stakes; the conservation initiative was simply
unable to compete in the circumstances.

In retrospect, engaging in a trial of strength with
a major logging concessionaire may appear
foolhardy; but when the PNG Biodiversity
Programme was designed, such an approach was
considered necessary to shift the goalposts for
conservation.

Though the project was terminated by its
sponsors in August 1996, it is important that the
lessons learned in New Ireland are shared widely
among conservation advocates, donor bodies and
other stakeholders with an interest in biodiversity
conservation.

A second Biodiversity Programme-funded
ICAD initiative is being piloted in the Bismarck
mountains and Ramu flood-plain. The lessons
learned from the New Ireland experience have
been incorporated into the design of that
initiative.

As most of the ICADPs (see Box) are in their
infancy, it is too early to draw conclusive lessons
from them. The complexity and risk inherent in
the ICAD process make detailed prior
“prescriptions” meaningless. Nonetheless, the
importance of sharing information on methods
and outcomes cannot be overstressed.
Collaboration is important for skills development,
to reduce the learning curve faced by conservation
workers, and to quickly establish the features of
successful conservation initiatives. To this end, it is
important to take stock periodically of critical
issues and challenges facing ICADPs.

This document aims at making such a
contribution by providing a snapshot of issues that
appear, at least at this point in time, to be
pertinent to the task of pursuing development as a
conservation tool. Although some of the general
issues pertaining to ICADPs have been
documented, an assessment of the issues as they
relate to Papua New Guinea is needed. A number
of factors present obstacles to the successful
realisation of [CAD objectives and it is important
that these be better understood. Some of the
challenges faced are unique to Melanesia; others
will no doubt be familiar to conservation
practitioners working elsewhere.

Part 1 of this report provides a short
introduction to the ICAD approach and
methodologies. An overview of the initiative is
then provided (Part 2), followed by a historical
review of the Lak project (Part 3). Key activities
are then examined in greater depth (Part 4) before
the lessons learned are explored (Part 5). This
review provides an in-depth analysis of the Lak
ICAD project activities and is presented in a spirit
of frankness and transparency. Many of the
problems encountered by the project and the
lessons that stem from them have only become
evident with the benefit of hindsight.

The report documents the power of the forces
waged against conservation. These are daunting.
Time was not on the Lak ICAD project's side.
Though the project might have taken a different
approach to engaging in social relations locally,
particularly with regard to community
participation and to awareness and advocacy, it is
unlikely in retrospect that these would have been
sufficient to secure a conservation win—if that
win is measured in terms of the establishment of a
‘protected area and the replacement of
conventional logging with more ecologically

favourable land uses. The Bismarck—Ramu
initiative has adopted a completely different
approach to education, to community
participation, and to community entry; but that
project has the luxury of time as the area of
interest is not being sought after for logging.
Conservationists have yet to find an approach
that works in the lowland tropical forest areas
coveted by logging concerns.

1.2 Integrated Conservation
and Development Projects
(ICADPs)

e Traditional approaches to the management
of protected areas have been found, for various
reasons, to be flawed. The problem is that such
strategies tended to exclude people from protected
sites, depending on punitive measures to achieve
management objectives (Wells et al. 1992).
Conservationists have come to realise that,
especially in the developing world, stable
conservation will be difficult to achieve without the
support of local communities. Large protected areas
are hard to police, making it difficult to control
“illegal” activities such as poaching and fuelwood
collection. The task of providing security is made
harder when local communities are hostile to
conservation efforts. As Wells et al. (1992) argue:
.... communities next to protected area(s)
frequently bear substantial costs—as a result of lost
access—while receiving little in return. Local
residents, who tend to be poor ... often perceive
protected areas as restricting their ability to make a
living. It is not surprising that the pressures of
growing populations and unsustainable land use
practices outside protected area boundaries
frequently lead to illegal and destructive
encroachment. (p. 2)

Many protected areas in developing countries
were established by colonial authorities, who took
property rights from local communities. In other °
countries, notably India, sanctuaries were typically
established as hunting reserves for the ruling
classes. In both instances, local communities were
excluded from lands which were later accorded
status as protected areas. As human populations
expand, and the natural resources required to fulfil
basic needs are depleted in the periphery of
protected areas, pressure on these areas inevitably

Race for the Rainforest

increases. Local communities come to resent the
restrictions placed on their entry to protected
zones for grazing, fuelwood collection, hunting,
gathering and the harvest of minor forest produce.
Risking penalties, they all too often infringe

management rules. As Brown and Wyckoff (1992) .

argue,
~ ++. when people are denied access to resources to
‘y which they have had access under traditional
"systems of tenure and resource use, conservation
policy runs the risk of backfiring over the long
term. (p. 24)

The problem is that the benefits from
conservation tend to be reaped by tourists, the
urban elite and society more broadly, while the
opportunity costs—in terms of forgone uses of the
resource—tend to be borne by local communities
(Wells 1992). The mismatched distribution of
costs and benefits tends to cause a disincentive for
conservation at the local level.

India, for instance, which has a relatively good
recent record in protecting endangered species,
faces rising human-animal conflicts. A policy of
exclusion has led to conflicts between protected
area authorities and local communities, resulting
in an increase in poaching and incursions into
national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. This point

is emphasised by Gadgil and Guha (1993):

© In displacing villagers without proper
rehabilitation, prohibiting traditional hunting and
gathering, and exposing villages on the periphery
to the threat of crop damage, cattle lifting and
manslaughter (by large animals) the parks are, as
they stand, inimical to the interests of the poorer
sections of agrarian society ... The dogma of total
protection can have tragic consequences.

(pp. 234-235)

Integrated conservation and development
projects aim at building linkages between the
welfare objectives of local communities and
biodiversity conservation goals by providing

communities with development support. Such
projects had their origins in Africa and Asia, where
conservation practitioners, recognising the
pointlessness of a policy of exclusion, began
working with local communities on park borders
to provide literacy education, water supply, micro-
business development, health care and other forms
of social and economic development support.!

ICAD projects may cover a range of activities
with vastly different scales of operation, with
different development/conservation philosophies,
and varying approaches to community
participation, education and conservation
advocacy. Project proponents tend to have widely
divergent views regarding what constitutes
“appropriate” development support. These range
from models that provide support for community-
led development to externally driven models. The
former provide technical assistance, education,
training and extension for activities fostered and
implemented by communities themselves, where
the communities dedicate human and financial
capital for development endeavours. With
externally driven models, projects may provide
communities with development “hardware”,
including seed finance for small businesses,
funding services and negotiating development

2.1 Introduction

Papua New Guinea may contain between
5 and 7 per cent of the world’s species (Miller et
al. 1994). As a result of isolation and evolutionary
radiation, a significant proportion of species are
endemic to the region. This diversity is a bounty
to the nation, supporting the productive economy
and providing essential ecological functions.
However, because many species have restricted
biogeographical distributions, they tend to have
low resilience thresholds in the face of human
pressures. Protecting the great wealth of species in
PNG in its entirety presents an immense set of
dilemmas for the conservation manager. In recent
years, pressures on forests have intensified as
human populations have increased, technologies
have advanced and the value systems of rural
communities have changed. These processes are
documented elsewhere (Morauta et al. eds 1982,
Sekhran & Miller eds 1994, Filer ed. 1997).
This section is intended to give readers who are
unfamiliar with the special problems that
conservationists face in Papua New Guinea, a
brief view of the larger picture.

2.1.1 Protected areas—
benefits and costs
Less than 2.8 per cent of Papua New

Guinea’s area has formal protected area status. The
present conservation system is not representative

of the country’s diverse habitats (some 80 per cent

of the present protected area network comprises

just three sites). On paper, the system consists of
four national parks on State land and some 30
other sites on both State and customary-owned
land. Most of these areas are small and
fragmented, and suffer from a lack of management
control. Pressures include overhunting,

& nsustainable subsistence use and, in some cases,

encroachment (UNDP 1993). Pressures are
growing at some sites from landowners who wish
to commit land to extractive development uses
incompatible with conservation objectives. Thus
the present protected area system hardly provides a
stable foundation for the conservation of the
nation’s biological diversity.

The decision to trial ICAD methods in PNG
stemmed from a recognition among conservation
planners that traditional national parks, that lock
up land, are not a practical conservation option,
given on-the-ground realities. Local communities
have come to see conservation and development as
conflicting pursuits (Filer 1995).4 Regardless of
their economic values, unless forests and their
biodiversity are seen to contribute to the material
development wants of communities, they are
increasingly likely to become targets of short-term
exploitation.

Many of the benefits of biological diversity are
non-monetary, creating a problem when local
communities measure “utility” and values purely
in monetary terms. A further problem is that
many benefits are diffuse, being reaped by
“society” as a whole or by future generations.

The costs of protection, on the other hand, are
disproportionately faced by (the present

2.1.2 The Lak experience

The Biodiversity Programme aims in part
to test the hypothesis that by demonstrating to
local communities that development and
conservation are not mutually exclusive goals, and
by assisting them to achieve their development
aspirations sustainably, a long-term framework for
stable conservation can be built. Though such a
hypothesis may prove correct in some parts of
Papua New Guinea, the Lak experience shows that
ICAD objectives are probably not achievable in
areas under immediate and sustained pressure;
conservation will always be difficult, but the blend
of social, economic, and political problems may
make the difficulties insurmountable in some
areas. As we will show in the “Lessons learned”
section of this document (Part 5), social
indicators, not political and biological criteria,
must guide the choice of project site. Critical
attention must also be paid to the design of an
appropriate approach to education and
community development. These were major
failings in the Lak approach that have been taken
into consideration at the Biodiversity Programme's

second ICAD site (the Bismarck—Ramu ICADP).

2.2 Factors mitigating against
(&\_ the conservation option

2.2.1 Property rights

4 As far as the conservation stakes are
concerned, Papua New Guinea presents a paradox.
Many economists and conservationists generally
see security over property as a pre-condition for
effective conservation management. It is argued
that in the absence of property rights, communities
have little or no stake in the long-term
management of ecosystems to assure their
productivity. Secure property rights, on the other
hand, are seen to provide communities with an

incentive for active management to protect
ecosystem functions and values. Many NGOs and
conservation advocates in countries such as India
argue persuasively that local communities must be
given a stake in land management if conservation
dilemmas are to be resolved. But the PNG
example shows that such a view is simplistic. Here,
more than 97 per cent of land falls under some
form of customary tenure, yet local communities
are opting against the conservation option. Why?
The answer is far from simple.

2.2.2 Cultural and social diversity

Papua New Guinea is one of the most
culturally diverse countries on earth with some
8000 traditionally autonomous tribal groups.
The country has 750 or more languages, falling
into some 180 language groups; these could be
considered as roughly similar to areas of shared
cultural affinities (Filer 1994). Given that the
country’s population accounts for just one-tenth of
one per cent of the world’s total population, this
diversity is extraordinary. It follows that social
systems, land tenure and cultural interpretations
of, and attachments to, the natural environment
vary greatly from region to region. As these are
such important factors of decision making at the
local level, this diversity greatly complicates the
search for a model approach to conservation.

+ Different methods of management need to be
developed to suit the social and cultural
characteristics of different areas.

2.2.3 Social and economic
environment

Papua New Guinea's great cultural and
social diversity also means that making
generalisations about the social and economic
landscape is difficult. It is worth keeping in mind
that the country has been catapulted from the
“stone age” (though not a primitive age; pre-
contact farming and subsistence systems were
complex and Papua New Guineans may have been
among the world’s first farmers) into the twentieth
century within a remarkably short space of time;
the central highlands was the last area to be
“contacted” but change has been rapid throughout
PNG. In this short duration, spanning less than
100 years for most communities, many traditions,
religious beliefs and world views have been

Race for the Rainforest

discarded as new cultural values and Christianity
have taken hold. In many cases, this has included
moral and religious groundings for conservation
practices.

Different communities have reacted to
changes in their social and economic environment
in vastly different ways. For instance, although a
few communities have prospered, becoming highly
productive participants in the formal economy,
others have not been so “fortunate”. The processes
of colonisation and the penetration of capitalism
have caused considerable dislocation among some
communities. Here, custom and modern ways sit
together in an uneasy alliance. This has often
manifested itself in terms of a breakdown in both
the traditional social control mechanisms and the
allegiance of younger clan members to their clan
traditions. The result has been growing
lawlessness, discordance within communities as
old and new values clash, and a general loss of
social cohesion. Significantly, bringing back past
conservation attachments and practices is difficult
because the social and economic transformation
wrought in many areas has been so complete. It is
difficult to return to the past.

2.2.4 Landownership

*< Despite these cultural, social and
economic problems, it may be argued that in some
respects at least, PNG has emerged from its
colonial experience more unscathed than other
developing countries. As already alluded to, the
country is unique in the developing world (outside
of Oceania at least) in that little land has been
“alienated” from its traditional owners. Though
there are significant variations in tenure
arrangements, land is generally controlled by
kinship groups, the membership of which is
determined through descent. (Land rights are
based mainly on inheritance but family size,
adoption, residence and use also play a part
[Holzknecht ed. 1994].) Rights-holding groups
control access to, and use of, the forest resource;
they thus dictate the future of the forest and must
be considered as the primary stakeholders in the
conservation arena.

It is worth pointing out, however, that though
forest and other above-soil resources are controlled
by landowning groups, these groups act as private
players in the allocation and use of these resources.
Forest use and access is determined by the

10

cost-benefit analysis of the group. Though this
may serve the local public good, it may not be in
the best public interests of the nation. (The
ideology of landownership and, in particular, the
perceived authority of the interests of
“landowners” complicate the debate on this
matter. PNG still needs to grapple with the issue
of external costs imposed by landowners in their
land use practices.)

Importantly, landowners’ interests do not
necessarily always prevail in landowner decisions;
individual power brokers—the traditional bigmen,
the educated elite, or wily manipulators—often
hijack decision-making processes to suit their own
personal interests. There is ample anecdotal
evidence that logging companies, in particular,
tend to enter into patron—client relations with a
few individuals in pursuing their interests in any
given community. Though outsiders may view
these transactions as “bribes”, to community
members they may be seen as engaging in social
relations. Cash payments and travel perks—
common forms of patronage—come with
considerable strings attached, as with traditional
forms of exchange in Melanesia:;Iypically,
recipients of patronage from timber concerns must
convince other clan members to commit land to
logging, campaign to get the government to issue a
timber permit, and ensure, once logging begins,
that operations run without hindrance.

Landowner groups, far from being cohesive
entities, tend to be fragmented. Groups are bound
together by a complex system of social relations
and obligations under custom and exchange
relationships, but there is a danger in viewing any
given group as a unitary category. The large
number of groups and group interests in any one
area makes finding a mutual agreement between
them difficult and poses a problem for
conservation managers. As stated, leadership is
often concentrated in the hands of a small group
of bigmen. But power is transitory and in time,
new leaders, with different political interests, are
likely to emerge (Filer pers. comm.). Thus
decisions may easily be overturned in the future as
the balance of power shifts within the community,
creating a problem of investment security for
outside players wanting to exploit development or
conservation opportunities.

For conservationists seeking a long-term
management compact with local communities, the
fact that current dealings, conducted in good faith,

Part 2: Biodiversity Conservation in Papua New Guinea: The Larger Picture

may be later overturned by the capricious
decisions of landowners imposes a high element of
risk. Importantly, this risk closes off opportunities
to capitalise on exciting new funding options for
conservation. Carbon swap initiatives, for
example, require that the recipients of investment
funds provide the service of carbon mitigation; for
forests, this usually means adherence to a strict
management regime. If application of the regime
cannot be guaranteed in the long term, then
investors will take their funds elsewhere (Stuart &

Sekhran 1997).

S.2.2.5 Welfare gap

A considerable welfare differential exists
between urban areas and enclave “development
areas” (such as mine sites), and that of remote
forest villages. Increasingly, many rural
communities see themselves as alienated from
mainstream development. The World Bank (1994)
estimates that 80 per cent of rural Papua New
Guineans have an annual per capita income of
USD 350, some 40 per cent of the national
average. Furthermore, using internationally
accepted comparative social indicators, PNG ranks
poorly. This includes infant mortality, maternal
mortality during childbirth, female illiteracy, and
the mean number of years of schooling (see
Gumoi & Sekhran 1994). Such differences NN
welfare do not go unnoticed at the local level and
play a major psychological role in “driving up”
local expectations of development.

2.2.6 Formal economy

The entry into the productive formal
economy of many rural communities, especially in
remote areas, is also hampered by a number of
factors. These include, among other things, a lack
of experience of the workings of the formal
economy, an acute shortage of appropriate skills,
conflicts between custom and small business
needs, and a lack of infrastructure. These
constraints tend to increase production and
marketing costs and make many cottage and
smallholder farm industries financially unattractive
or simply unprofitable.

The combination of the welfare gap and
constraints to participation in the formal sector
causes a great deal of frustration and
disempowerment among local communities. This

11

is providing an enormous incentive for many
communities to “hive off” forest resources to
loggers in return for development. Many
communities have come to see development as
gaining rent (Filer 1996) rather than as a process
of change that builds local capacity and the
productive economy. As Filer argues:
Papua New Guinea, which was once a nation of
gardeners, is now becoming a nation of gatekeepers
(to resources) and rent collectors ... Although there
may still only be a minority of the rural population
which actually does receive some form of resource
rent, the vast majority now seem to subscribe to
the belief that their land does contain some
valuable resource—whether gold, oil, diamonds or
the truly visible logs—and their only chance of
“development” lies in their share of the rent to be
collected from the extraction of these resources by
some multinational company.

2.2.7 Mineral resources development

The mining sector dominates Papua New
Guinea’s economic landscape, and its high-profile
operations provide a considerable display of wealth.
Though only a few communities have been “lucky”
enough to be gatekeepers to mineral and petroleum
deposits, their experience has helped shape
expectations in other areas. Filer (1996) continues:

The nation boasts a small number of “lucky strike”
communities ... the national government has seen
fit to “compensate” this particular group of
landowners with a constantly increasing share of
the resource rent which accrues from their own
project ... Such has been the publicity accorded
these acts of generosity and so widespread the
wanderings of company geologists, that the rest of
the rural population has been more or less
consumed in the anticipation of a mineral
dependent destiny.

2.2.8 Other resources, including forests

Where mineral resource development is not
an option locally, landowners are turning to other
resources, and especially their forest resources.

Thus an attitude that “development is rent”
has taken hold. Communities stress the “delivery”
of development benefits by outsiders in return for..,
gaining access to their resources, which they use as
leverage. Such a view of development is over-
simplified. This is especially so as income is being
channelled for immediate consumption rather

Race for the Rainforest

than investment in human and human-made
assets. The problem is that resource extraction
tends to preclude sustainable development
activities, by destroying the very resource base
needed to support them. At the same time,
extractive development projects tend to be short-
term; the fact that communities are not
capitalising on the returns to build their asset base
to widen oppertunities and choices in the future,
is shutting off future options. If community
development is interpreted, in the widest possible
way, as a process of meeting community
aspirations in the long term, the current situation
may be seen as being directly opposed to its
objectives.

To communities, the earnings from logging
projects may appear to represent a lucrative
“windfall”. Though it is difficult to determine the
proportion of the total earnings from logging that
communities receive (because logging operations
are rarely transparent, and because there is much
disagreement regarding the costs of operations in
PNG), a number of studies have hazarded guesses.
One study estimated that at 1994 prices, given a
harvest cost of K50 (USD 56 in 1994),
landowners receive a total of some 8.3 per cent of
gross benefits. This comprised royalties, associated
infrastructural benefits, returns to landowner
companies, and employment.

The Lak situation demonstrates that this may
be an overestimate. However, it is difficult to
know how much community bigmen receive
directly from logging patrons, or what actual
employment incomes are, and so it is impossible
to be exact. Assuming that the total amount
received from logging operations ranges between
5 and 8 per cent of gross income, for an export of
100,000 cubic metres a year, and an FOB timber
price of K150 per cubic metre, the local
community should earn between K750,000 and
K1,200,000 a year. Over a five-year operation, this
translates into sizeable sums. Given these amounts,
it is little wonder that conservation efforts appear
unattractive, as they provide only long-term
returns, and then only if community members
expend considerable labour effort.

2.2.9 Rental economy

The fascination of many Papua New
Guineans with rent collection may seem irrational
to outsiders, as they cannot understand when

communities pay little attention to awareness
programmes aimed at promoting the benefits of
long-term planning. But it is easy for outsiders to
forget that the monetisation of PNG’s rural
economy has been relatively recent. Most rural
communities still depend heavily on subsistence
practices to provide for sustenance. There are
explicit trade-offs between participation in the
formal sector and engaging in subsistence in terms
of labour time budgets. Unless productive
economic activities, such as cash cropping, are able
to provide the basis for sustenance—at the very
least in terms of generating sufficient income to
feed a household—communities cannot afford to
forgo their subsistence practices. In remote areas of
the country, constraints to productive-use activities
can be severe, and returns can be small for a lot of
work effort. The productive economy cannot
provide for the welfare of these communities,
especially when commodity prices are low.

The rental economy provides short-term
income for little labour investment and this
provides a compelling incentive to become a rent
collector. To this a rational outsider might argue,
“Perhaps, but what about the long term? Surely
communities can see that destruction of their
resource base heralds poorly for the future? Surely
they have seen that many communities, who have
engaged in logging in the past, have been left with
little to show for it in the end?”. As we shall see,
the Lak experience shows that communities are
not naive about the environmental costs of their
chosen land use option. But they often find it hard
to understand that these costs are long-term ones
(that is, that the forest will not necessarily heal
itself). The short-term costs appear like an
acceptable sacrifice for gaining access to a rental
income. This situation can be found throughout
Papua New Guinea. Indeed, in many cases
environmental damage may be used as a basis for
making compensation demands aimed at
increasing the rental take. After all, as gatekeepers
to the resource, landowners have a lot of
bargaining power.

Rural communities have been socialised into a
world view that is focused on the short term. In
the past, when people were controlled by the
environment, when life spans were short and
technologies simple, the focus on the short term
would not have been surprising. People living at
the “margin” rarely have the luxury of thinking
about the future. Life is a struggle against nature’s

Part 2: Biodiversity Conservation in Papua New Guinea: The Larger Picture

vagaries including drought, floods and even frost.
In this situation, people are likely to plan for their
short-term needs rather than those of the longer
term. Thus, the longer term consequences of
present decisions are poorly understood, or
ignored.

In earlier times, as the pace of ecological
change resulting from human—environment
interactions was slow, people’s understanding of
the determinants of that change, and their role in
affecting it, was poor. The belief that nature
controls people means that communities often
believe that they cannot destroy the forest in the
first place. The problem is that technological
change and population growth, which have altered
the human—environment balance and made it
easier for humans to affect the environment, have
changed faster than these attitudes. When coupled
with the new demand for cash, these factors are a
potent cocktail. As Orsak argues:

“Villagers” traditional experiences using forest

resources tell them that “nature controls people”,

not the other way around. Under that perception,
the notion that natural resources can be
permanently destroyed by people is a non-issue.

This leaves only one issue to weigh—how much

short-term benefit each competing activity offers.

Logging and other short-term extractive activities

will always offer more fast money, and so they win,

hands down. So long as these perceptions
dominate community thinking, nature

conservation is likely to be embraced only as a

short-term novelty with little hope of being

sustained. (pers. comm.)°

2.3 The stakeholders
x There are five main stakeholders involved

in forest resources development in Papua New
Guinea—forest communities, conservation
proponents, the State, timber concerns, and the
“technocrats”.

2.3.1 Forest communities

Forest communities are selling forest
access rights to loggers as a means of obtaining
quick cash and social services. They see this to be

the best way of resolving their concerns regarding
economic exclusion. The level of rental incomes is
increasingly used by communities as a measure of
progress. In contrast, conservation and sustainable
development initiatives, because they provide
future benefits, or ones that are intangible and
diffuse, are seen to conflict with the development
wants of communities. This message is constantly
being reinforced by many politicians and by some
developers.

2.3.2 Conservation proponents

The problem facing conservationists in
Papua New Guinea is that in the present social and
political climate, communities are not committed
to “self-help” (which they see as burdensome) in
pursuing their development ambitions. The
danger is that when conservation is presented to
them as an option, they equate this with the word

“project” and the word “project” with “rent”.

' Conservation becomes just another means of
earning rent; there is little belief in conservation as
a land use (Orsak pers. comm.). This was very
much the problem in Lak and the ICAD project
was unsuccessful in shifting these attitudes.

Most ICAD projects have a philosophy of
development that places the responsibility for
delivery on the communities themselves. They shy
away from the rental economy, arguing that only
by participating in the productive sphere, making
sacrifices and learning to be disciplined, can
communities achieve successful long-term
development. Thus the projects focus on self-help,
providing the missing ingredients of development
such as technical assistance, literacy training and,
perhaps, micro-credit. None of the current ICAD
projects has sufficient funds to do otherwise
though the Lak project was forced to attempt this.

Another major issue for conservation is
that the PNG State has little bargaining power
when it comes to influencing landowner decisions
regarding land use. In the past, the interference of
the State in local affairs was accepted (to a point)
by communities in return for services, but its role

Again, however, it is the politics of patronage
that in part causes political apathy towards
conservation. More than half of the sitting
members in PNG’s national Parliament lose their
seats in any given election under a “first past the
post” system. Margins are small. The juxtaposition
of the Westminster electoral system and custom
makes for tough politics. Constituents tend to
support candidates in return for generosity or
future patronage, and not on policy issues. In
short, politicians must service their constituents’
material wants or risk being unseated. This is a
catalyst for corruption, causing many politicians to
seek patronage from industries, including the
forest sector. Developers in turn provide patronage
in exchange for favours. Increasingly, policy is
being geared towards meeting the interests of an
influential group of developers who have
cemented their power through dealings with
unscrupulous politicians. Though there is much
consternation in the press about corruption, there
has been relatively little debate as to its causes.
This is also true at the grassroots level where
communities condemn corruption while
simultaneously demanding favours in return for
extending political support.

Needless to say, the State is subverting its role
of protecting the wider national interest in
biodiversity conservation, to promote logging.
Though theoretically, irrespective of landowner
interests, the State is able to preserve land from
industrial logging under the National Forest Plan,
it is unwilling to do so. The authority of
landowner interests is used as an excuse. In this
situation, conservation is unlikely to be achieved
unless a moral dimension is introduced into the
debate. As we will argue repeatedly in this
document, this can only come about through
education.

2.4 Forest harvesting practices ~*~"

(¢

Sf A major problem is that forest harvesting
practices in Papua New Guinea have not
improved. Though conservationists accept that
logging is a necessary industry—to provide vital
jobs, income and tax revenue—they are appalled
by the type of logging practices used in the field.
These are characterised by a notable lack of
forward planning, a lack of skilled personnel to
operate the harvesting equipment, and a disregard
for the most basic environmental safeguards. The
result is that the future productivity of the forest
is being eroded.

Between 30 and 70 per cent of the residual
trees in logging concessions die in the post-harvest
period (Cameron & Vigus 1993). These trees
should comprise the future harvest. Forest trees
tend to be bound together by vines in the canopy;
sound harvesting practices dictate that vines be cut
prior to harvest and stems allowed to rot. Failure
to do this means inevitably that non-targeted trees
are felled together with merchantable trees. Also,
logging in buffer zones is common (including
along stream beds), conservation sites are ignored,
and larger than necessary gap formations are
created. These gaps tend to be colonised by vines,
retarding the regrowth of trees. The compaction
of soil by heavy logging equipment also retards
regeneration.

The Forestry Guidelines were to have resolved
these issues; but stricter planning and low-impact
operations have direct (incremental) costs. The
logging industry has opposed any change to the
current situation because of this.

3.2.1 Geographical features

The province of New Ireland is the second
largest island in the Bismarck archipelago, an area
directly to the north-east of the PNG mainland.
Being some 350 kilometres in length and yet
rarely exceeding 20 kilometres in width, New
Ireland is a distinctive sliver-shaped island. At the
southern end of the island is a distinctive “bulge”,
where the island reaches its broadest width—some
45 kilometres. Topographically, this southern
extremity consists of two mountain ranges, the
Hans Meyer Range and the Verron Range, which
rise abruptly out of the sea and reach a height of
over 2300 metres. It is these mountains that
provide the biogeographic character for southern
New Ireland, a character that ts distinct from the
rest of New Ireland and the Bismarck archipelago.
The southern part of New Ireland is divided
longitudinally along the central ridge of the
mountains into two provincial government
electorates: Kandas (West Coast) and Lak
(East Coast).

The Lak area is dominated by mountainous
topography. A unique geographical feature is the
Weitin Valley, a rift valley produced by the action
of colliding tectonic plates. This feature, when
combined with the effects of island biogeography,
has produced unique forests which “... are among
the most complex, highest, and least disturbed of
any in PNG’s islands region” (Beehler 1994).?

3.2.2 Biological diversity

Biologically, Lak is a diverse area that
contains a valuable genetic storehouse. The area
boasts a high rate of species endemism and the
forest composition and wildlife communities are
unique, though little understood (Beehler 1994).
Endemic species of particular note include the
White-naped Lory, New Ireland Friarbird, New

Ireland Myzomela, Long-tailed Drongo and _
Hunsteins Mannikin. Also of importance is the
widespread presence of the Leatherback Turtle
which comes ashore to breed at several sites in
Lak. The New Britain Flying Fox (Pteropus
gilliaridorum), considered exceptionally rare, was
collected during the Biodiversity Rapid Survey in
1994 (see Section 3.3.3), the first time it has been
collected since 1958. The flora of Lak is also the
focus of considerable biological interest with the
five predominant forest types: coastal limestone
forest, interior riverine lowland forest, hill forest,
montane mossy forest and mountain-top
myrtaceous heath forest, providing a rich
vegetative mosaic. Of particular note are the small
pockets of “giant forest”, consisting primarily of
Pometia and reaching over 50 metres in height.
Importantly, the conservation significance of the
Lak area is rapidly increasing as virtually all of the

lowland forests in the New Guinea Islands region

have been logged or are targeted for future logging
in the NFA National Forest Plan (Hedemark pers.

comm.),!9

Clan structure and interrelationships

The structure of interrelationships between clans is
immensely complicated but is based on a pattern
found throughout the New Guinea Islands region
where clans are classified as belonging to either the
big bird (sea eagle) or small bird (fish hawk)
moieties. Clan subgroupings are based on kinship.
They comprise members of an extended family who
are related through the female line. Children belong
to their mother’s clan which is different from their
father’s since, according to traditional law, marriages
can only take place between members of different
moieties (Powdermaker 1932). In the Lak area,
cross-cousin marriages were regarded as a way of
safeguarding the land by uniting family and clan
interests through marriage (McGregor 1993).
Members of the same moiety function as a unit
only for ceremonial purposes such as food exchanges
at marriages and births, or in the performance of
death or initiation rites. On such occasions moieties
cut across the lines of kinship because the mother
and children of any family will belong to a
different moiety from that of their father
(Powdermaker 1932).

3.2.5 Economic and social isolation

The Lak have nicknamed their area
laskona,'? which is both a geographical description
of their relationship to other New Irelanders and
an oblique reference to the fact that the Lak
people see themselves as isolated and remote and
describe themselves as economically poor and
socially disadvantaged when compared to outsiders
(Waisale pers. comm.). Lak is probably among the

least developed areas within the province. Factors
such as the distance from the provincial capital
(Kavieng), the poor transport and
communications infrastructure, the small and
scattered nature of the population, and the rugged
exposed nature of the coastline combine to isolate
the area in every sense. The level of government
services available to the Lak community is low
although there are four community schools, a
health centre and three aid posts, a district services
officer and an officer from the Department of
Primary Industries.

3.3.14 ICAD Project Base

Construction of the ICADP Base began
in May 1995. The construction of the operational
base was seen by the community as a tangible sign
of the project’s commitment to the area. Up to
200 visitors a day toured the construction site and

informally adopted as the main project operations

communities asked project staff for assistance with
transport to the health centre at Silur. Many families
spent considerable time each week finding

rapidly following the beginning of logging operations
upstream, and was thought to be a major cause of
disease.

Project staff devised a system of water reticulation
for the villages worst affected by poor water quality. A
donor was found to provide assistance. The installation
of water tanks in each village, with water piped in
underground from a safe source, promised clean water
and significant time savings as women would no longer
need to fetch water from distant creeks. However,
when this system was presented to the communities,
with project staff offering the equipment and technical
advice and the village expected to provide labour,
inevitably the first question was, “How much will ICAD
pay us to dig the ground for the pipe?” (Alois Topot

pers. comm.). Although the communities saw value in
the communal water system, their recent experiences
with the cash economy and interaction with the
logging company would not allow them, as individuals,
to work for the community good. “We won’t work for
free, we are not fools. Only fools would work for free
like slaves” (Topot pers. comm.).

record statistics. The scheme has been very successful

infections, preventative treatment of malaria, and
assistance with childbirth. The ICADP, as part of the

4.1 The Lak Forestry Initiative

Two innovative and ambitious solutions
developed for the Lak ICAD project were the Lak
Conservation Area Sustainable Forestry Project
and the Carbon Initiative.

4.1.1 The Sustainable Forestry Project

~ Reduced impact logging (RIL) can
provide many of the benefits of conventional
logging, but at a fraction of the ecological cost.
The ICADP conceived the Lak Conservation Area
Sustainable Forestry Project (LCASFP) as a
demonstration model to show that logging can be
conducted in an ecologically sensible way. When
coupled with active species management measures,
it was hoped that conservation activities and
industrial forestry operations could be integrated
in one area. A system of zoning was envisaged to
dedicate land to various uses including low-impact
logging, sustainable agriculture, and undisturbed
zones for strict species protection. Incentive and
transfer mechanisms were to have been developed
to make this attractive to landowners. In this
section we answer the following questions: What
was the Sustainable Forestry Project? How was the
project to be managed? How was the project to be
funded? and How much could it potentially have
earned? A brief description of the Lak Carbon
Swap initiative, pursued in parallel to the LCASFP,
is then provided.

Objectives

The aim of the Lak Sustainable Forestry Project
was to establish an ecologically sustainable, low-
impact forest harvesting operation that would
provide a sustainable livelihood for local
communities and, in doing so, show that

41

environmental protection can be profitable. The
project was in part modelled on a reduced impact
logging initiative in Sabah, Malaysia (Putz 1994,
Pinard 1994). The technical aspects of the
proposed Lak prototype were developed by

Dr Frans Arentz of ANUTECH in Canberra, a
former editor of the International Tropical Timber
Organization's (ITTO’s) Tropical Forest Update
magazine, and a forester with many years of
experience in Papua New Guinea. The proposal
recognised that there would always be a degree of
environmental impact with any timber harvesting
operation; however, the scale of impact can be
managed through careful planning, through
providing training for field operators in the use of
equipment, and by the application of a set of strict
operating guidelines. These include guidelines for
skid trail planning and marking, directional
felling, marking of residuals, tree felling, skidding,
log landing establishment and post-harvest
operations (Arentz pers. comm.).

Components

The Sustainable Forestry Project had a number of
components. These included (Sekhran et al. 1997):
(1) Selective logging of natural forest, using
internationally accepted low-impact logging
techniques. Proposed activities included the
preparation of logging and road plans,
upgrading of logging roads, and planning and
building snig tracks and log landings. Pre-fell
vine cutting and directional felling were to be
applied as harvest methods, and trees were to
be skidded using tracked rather than wheeled
vehicles. Particular attention was to have been
paid to felling to reduce overall damage and to
improve the efficiency of operations.
Application of the guidelines would have

Race for the Rainforest

required the training of chainsaw and skidding
equipment operators and strict supervision.
Operations were to have been halted during
periods of heavy rain to reduce disturbance

to soil.

An Environmental Impact Assessment
(EIA) was to have been commissioned to
provide an independent environmental audit
of the project, including measures for waste
disposal, soil management and for dealing
with social impacts. Finally, post-harvest
assessments and the establishment of
permanent growth plots were planned for the
purposes of assessing regeneration. Follow-up
management needs were expected to be
limited owing to the use of low-impact
techniques. However, it was accepted that the
application of the proposed methods would
need to be independently authenticated and
that a pro-active approach to silvicultural
management would be necessary to account
for unexpected needs.

Rehabilitation. Proposed activities involved
rehabilitation of areas previously logged by
conventional methods using enrichment
planting and the establishment of plantations.
At the time the forestry initiative was
announced, some 17,000 hectares of accessible
forest resources containing over 350,000 cubic
metres were thought to remain in Lak.
Without a forestry inventory, the ICADP was
forced to deduce these figures from Forest
Service data. (These were considered to be
inaccurate.*°) The original proposal called for
a harvest of 10,000 cubic metres a year,
though this was later revised upwards to
improve the financial cost—benefit equation
for the project. Harvesting at such a rate
would have exhausted the timber resource in
less than forty years, and possibly much
earlier, given suspicions regarding the accuracy
of existing stand volume data. .

As already stated, conventional logging in
Lak had caused significant damage to residual

trees; gaps had been colonised by vines and
regrowth was being suppressed. It is unlikely
that commercial logging in these areas would
be feasible for at least 60 or perhaps 100 years
(Arentz pers. comm.). The danger in leaving
this land in an unproductive state was that it
would provide an incentive for salvage logging
or permanent conversion to agriculture, if the
opportunity arose. Measures to rehabilitate
these areas and bring them into a productive
state for timber harvesting were thus
considered necessary. In some areas,
enrichment planting would have sufficed as a
management intervention; this would have
involved vine clearance to enable seedlings to
grow and residual trees to recover from
physiological stress. Other possible measures
in these areas included site tending, and the
ripping of log landings and other areas where
soils had been compressed.

At other sites, enrichment management
measures would clearly have been insufficient
to return logged-over areas into a productive
state (Arentz pers. comm.) In these areas, the
project proposed to establish plantations of
fast-yielding species. Some 2000 hectares were
to have been developed for plantations under
the initial proposal, though the actual area to
be planted would have been determined by
the planned forest inventory and assessments
of damage. Activities proposed under this
component included the establishment of
nurseries, salvage logging and the marking,
planting and tending of trees.

Downstream processing of round logs to increase
local value added. An existing two-blade
circular sawmill on site, purchased by the
Metlak Development Corporation but hardly
used, was to have been commissioned for
milling purposes. Additional equipment
would have been required, for milling, drying
and dressing. However, development of this
component hinged on a fuller feasibility
investigation. In particular, there was

It is important that ICAD projects conduct dealings

' with landowning groups rather than with local

landowning groups control access to, and use of, land
and resources; it makes sense that these groups be

of conservation management compacts. Not all
individuals in a community are landowners, and the
danger in dealing with the body-politic of villagers is
that rights-holders may not be sufficiently involved.
This provides little long-term stability—the very real
risk exists of dealings being contested at a later stage
(Holzknecht pers. comm.).

To an outside agency, however, local land tenure
practices and social groupings may appear bewildering.
A means of mapping membership of land groups, and
organising them as legally recognised entities (with
recognition given to the group’s claims to land and
resources), needs to be found. Outside parties,
including conservation agencies, could then enter into
dealings with these groups (with these dealings
recognised by law).

The Land Groups Incorporation Act (1974) provides
such a mechanism. Incorporation involves a process
starting with the identification of customary social
units and ending with their registration as
Incorporated Land Groups (ILGs). Broadly, the
_ process involves:

(a) assisting landowners to determine group
membership criteria and to prepare genealogies;

(b) assisting bona fide landowner groups to develop
constitutions that govern internal decision making
(within the ILGs); and

(c) incorporating the groups under the provisions of
the Act (Holzknecht ed. 1994).

ILGs are not a panacea for conservation; their main
purpose is to provide more “security” in landowner
dealings. By targeting ICAD project activities at land
groups, the benefits of projects will be received by
community members who control land access and use.
In practice, however, the State has little capacity to
enforce dealings with land groups, if these groups
break negotiated compacts (Whimp 1997). Thus, the
very real danger exists that conservation compacts—
that restrict resource use for a conservation end—will
later be discarded by landowners when faced with a
better “offer”. This provides little surety for
conservation and is a significant hurdle to establishing
a market-based approach to resource management
(such as through carbon swaps). In the long term, the
service being purchased (conservation, or carbon
mitigation) by the investor simply cannot be
guaranteed. In the end, conservation projects in Papua
New Guinea will have to rely on building a belief in
conservation and self-help among landowners in order
to achieve results.

To improve the financial bottom line of

Concept

Papua New Guinea's tropical forests are an
important bank of carbon—fixing carbon dioxide
through photosynthesis and storing carbon in
plant tissues. In a state of natural equilibrium,
these forests fix and release roughly equal
proportions of carbon. Any changes in this
equilibrium, through forest disturbance, shatters
this delicate carbon balance, leading to a net
release of carbon and other greenhouse gases (such
as nitrous oxide and methane). Globally, 22 per
cent of all greenhouse gases from human causes
come from the decay of dead wood after logging
and from forest burning (Faeth et al. 1994).

Developed countries are currently moving to
meet their obligations under the Framework
Convention on Climate Change (FCCC).47
The Convention makes provision for “joint
implementation” as a mechanism whereby
industries in polluting nations, forced with
reducing greenhouse gas emissions levels, can
make carbon mitigation investments in other
countries where such emissions reductions are
more efficient and cost effective. A number of
power utilities in developed countries have
invested in reduced impact logging projects and
afforestation in developing countries, as
compensation for their contribution to the
anthropogenic greenhouse gas build-up. It is often
cheaper for these companies to pay to avoid
damage elsewhere than to make cuts in their own
emissions or to switch fuels. Also, the developed
countries, as they move to meet their obligations
under the FCCC, will need to take more stringent
steps in the future to regulate industries
responsible for emitting greenhouse gases. Utility
companies hope that “carbon offsets” will be taken
into account when such steps are taken (so that
the final measures are less punitive than otherwise
might be the case).

In PNG, logging practices needlessly cause
excess damage to forests, leading to greater than
necessary decay of biomass and the consequent
release of excess greenhouse gases. Alternative
logging practices, aimed at reducing these impacts,
are being implemented in Sabah, Malaysia, among
other countries.

Structure

became clear that the “senior partners” often
lacked the skills and experience necessary to make
resource allocation decisions of the size and
complexity being forced on them by the short-

Committee in fulfilling its role, several problems

Problems

Payment of ICAD Committee members

An early problem that hampered the performance
of the Committee was the issue of the payment of
Committee members. The LCAMC was a new

Members of the ICAD Committee were also

5.1 Introduction

This section records the lessons learned
from the Lak ICAD project. The lessons are by no
means exhaustive and future commentators will
no doubt add to them. A number of issues
reappear; this is necessary in order to assess their
influence on the project from different angles. For
instance, the dual issues of dependency and
community motivation are frequently discussed;
the issues are dealt with in the context of different
lessons. The various lessons are not ranked in
order of importance; what is important is their
combined effect.

The lessons provide some pointers to future
ICAD initiatives in terms of structuring project
interventions. The Bismarck—Ramu ICADP has
been designed to take on board many of the issues
raised, though, to be fair, there are as yet no
answers to many of the problems. Most of the
current ICAD projects in PNG are facing similar
problems to those faced in Lak; the difference is
that in Lak, because logging had already begun,
the problems were more acute than in other areas.
The authors have tried to be transparent about the
problems faced in the hope that this will
contribute to the body of literature and knowledge
on conservation activities in Papua New Guinea.
If conservation is to progress, it is important that
practitioners sift the evidence from the field to
redefine their approaches to project management.

chances of success are higher.

Political criteria for site selection
The logging contractor had established itself in

Lak two years before conservation proponents
appeared on the scene. This gave it certain
advantages; the company had already developed
extensive social and economic relations with the
community and, in particular, with the power
brokers within it, when the ICAD initiative was
first mooted. Moreover, Lak landowners had been
campaigning for a logging project for at least eight
years prior to the actual start of harvesting. The
reasons for this have already been given. Briefly
restated, many community members saw logging
as their sole opportunity to participate in the
development arena. This fact was noticed by
outside commentators. Colin Filer, a member of
the Task Force on Environmental Planning in
Priority Forest Areas, made the following
comment on his return from Lak in 1990:

Our conservationist friends in the developed world
sometimes seem to imagine that Papua New
Guinean villagers resemble the Indians of the
Amazon rainforest—people living in simple
harmony with Mother Nature, for whom
“development” is a menace imposed by outsiders.
If these people went to Lak, they would be in for a
big shock. It would probably be difficult to find
another place in PNG where local landowners were
more insistent on the need to have their trees cut
down as soon as possible ... The landowners have
long believed, or have been led to believe, that
logging was their one and only way of escaping
what (they) constantly called the “suffering” of
their backwardness. (Filer 1990a, p. 72)

Yet, for project management and staff, the

logging interests resulted in these words of caution

position is difficult to contest. (However, the

When the Biodiversity Programme was

the blame for forest mismanagement on logging
companies. Given this, the Programme set out to
challenge the companies’ forest management
practices and dominance in the land use stakes.
Though this may now be seen as an unachievable
act of bravado, at the time it appeared an essential
strategy in the effort to secure conservation goals.

The Lak project was chosen for a combination
of biological and political reasons. The area was
less species-rich than the New Guinea mainland,
but contained endemic species and served as a
refuge for species unique to the Bismarck
archipelago, but threatened elsewhere. The
political reason was the de facto “mandate” to
make a stand against logging. However, socio-
economic factors were ignored. There was
insufficient analysis of community thinking and of
the factors influencing decision making; many of
the problems that the project subsequently faced
stem from these early days.

Assessing social feasibility

The project did not develop a framework for social
feasibility assessments at Lak (such a framework is
being developed for the first time in PNG by the
Bismarck—Ramu Project initiative). In hindsight,
there were a number of warning signals. One
commentator questioned the communities
motivation for self-help after noting the absence
of trade stores in an area, despite the fact that
logging was providing windfall income

(Ellingson pers. comm.).

A critical lesson learned from Lak, reinforcing
what has already been said (Brown & Holzknecht
1993), is that though biological criteria may be
used as a basis for identifying broad areas of
interest (of high conservation value) for
conservation interventions, social criteria must
dictate the choice of specific sites.

Social feasibility at the Bismarck—Ramu ICAD Project

The PNG National Research Institute, on behalf of the
Biodiversity Programme, is piloting a Social Feasibility
Study in the Bismarck-Ramu area. The purpose is, first,
to collect basic social, economic and human
geographical information on the area of interest, and
second, to assess the efficacy of the project’s
community entry approach and the receptivity of local
communities to it. The study will assist project
proponents to evaluate project feasibility and (given

a decision to remain in the area) to design project
activities to deal with local issues. The aim is to tailor
the project to suit the local human geographical and
social landscape and to increase its capacity to
respond to emergent problems.

The study will build a criteria set for assessing
social feasibility. This includes evaluating community
motivation, collaboration potential, opportunities and
capacity for income generation, and basic social
mapping. Information is being collected on
population, demographic change and migration

In the past, ICADPs elsewhere have generally
been applied in buffer zones abutting traditional
conservation areas. Local communities have
usually been excluded from these areas, causing
them to resent conservation activities. In these
situations, ICADPs (the carrot) serve as a

conservation managers must try to negotiate a

largely depend on the cost-benefit analysis of
communities, and of the power brokers within

for conservation purposes, when they already enjoy
unrestricted access to the resource, as is the case in
PNG. Enforcement is hard. Communities must in
effect regulate their own activities; a considerable
change of attitude is required for this to work.

Lessons from other development projects

A good starting point would be to record the
lessons learned from past development projects.
For instance, a number of Integrated Rural
Development Projects (IRDPs) have been tried in
rural PNG. Yet there has been remarkably little
sharing of lessons between ICADPs and IRDPs.
Though ICAD projects are obviously more

catalyst of appropriate development is similar.

Likewise, the mining companies have invested
heavily in community development programmes.

studied. (However, the lessons learned here will

5.1.2 Community entry

The style and substance of an ICAD

project’s initial contact with local

communities will have implications for
its subsequent ability to handle the issue of
“dependency”. Many communities equate
projects with opportunities for gaining rent;
projects that obviously display wealth are
likely to reinforce these ideas. (Exhibitions
of wealth include the display of project
equipment, entry to the area by helicopter, or
consuming imported food on patrol.) ICAD
projects must challenge these notions.
Dependency attitudes need to be confronted
and self-help ones encouraged. Project field
staff need to set an example; their behaviour
in the field will have a bearing on the ICAD
project's own ability to challenge certain
community attitudes. As the required

community entry approach can be very
different from the normal mode of
implementing community development and
conservation projects, a careful strategy will
need to be framed and accepted by project
management and staff before field
operations begin.

ICAD projects must aim at building self-
reliance at the local level. Rather than “doing”
development, they must provide catalytic support
for it. (The Biodiversity Programme has never
formally articulated a development “philosophy”;
however, the Programme does not have the funds
to replace the role of government by delivering
services, or to release villagers from responsibilities
for their own development by implementing
projects on their behalf; the Programme can only
realistically provide technical assistance, training
and other such catalytic support for local-level
initiatives.) The problem is that, as already stated,
many rural communities in Papua New Guinea
have come to see their development as the
responsibility of outsiders.

The key issue is that ICAD projects can never
compete with large developers in terms of
providing landowners with opportunities for
gaining short-term rent. If the potential for rent
capture remains the basis for decision making at
the local level, then ICAD projects will almost
certainly fail. Thus certain local values and views
about development need to be challenged.

It is now clear that ICAD projects must tread
carefully when initially contacting villagers in areas
of interest for conservation. Community entry,
which refers to this contact stage, involves a careful
process of engagement, of trust building,
information gathering, awareness raising,
education and other activities. This stage is critical
for ICAD projects. The challenge for projects is to
establish durable relationships with community
stakeholders; to achieve this, the power imbalances
between conservation proponents and villagers
must be taken into account. Project activities that
display affluence—for instance ferrying staff from
site to site by helicopter, or providing project staff
with expensive imported food—are likely only to
reinforce high expectations at the local level.

spin-offs. As the Lak BIORAP involved eminent
expatriate scientists working on tight time schedules,
and as the area was inaccessible, the study team
made wide use of helicopters for transport. Food was
brought in from outside the area. Local people were
hired as porters and guides and were exposed to the
“affluent” lifestyles of the scientific workers. The

_ effect was to reinforce high expectations of kago
(cash, consumer goods and services) locally. The
presence of expatriates, the use of helicopters, and
other factors, reinforced the notion that the ICAD
project was wealthy; villagers saw little reason why
this wealth should not be shared with them. Thus,
inadvertently, the ICAD project reinforced local
dependency rather than challenging it.

Project proponents should not underestimate the
sensitivity of landowners to the detection of signs of
wealth. Indeed, the simple presence of an expatriate
can indicate that a project is able to deliver more
than it is offering.

Community entry needs to be seen as a
separate project activity and should be carefully
planned and carried out. Patrol members need to
be briefed on appropriate approaches to

the field. Based on the lessons learned in Lak, the
Bismarck—Ramu ICADP has developed a
participatory approach to community entry (see
Grant 1996). The methods used, which show
promise in field tests, may be adapted for use at
other project sites.

ICAD projects are based on the notion

that by providing local communities with

/ support for development, a direct link

problems with providing material incentives
within an ICAD framework. Successful
community-based income-generation
projects require a wide range of ingredients;
many of these may be absent locally.
Although some of the problems facing such
projects may be resolved in the longer term,
community expectations of “quick fix”
development, and their ability to obtain it
(that is, by charging resource rents to
loggers), may preclude the search for
solutions. The problem is that conventional
logging may destroy the very resource base
required by the alternatives—making them
simply unworkable.

It is now clear that ICADPs must invest
heavily in education to transform
communities’ ideas about development.
Unless communities come to redefine their
strategies for seeking development in ways
that ICAD projects can support, then
“conservation through development”
initiatives will continue to fail.

The National Forestry and Conservation

options. (This may stem from a widely held idea
that Papua New Guineans in the past lived in a
mystical state of harmony with nature [see Filer
1995]; this view assumes that these attachments
may be called up again for present-day
conservation.) In practice, this assumption
simplifies both the decision-making processes
of rural communities and the reality on the
ground for community development and
conservation efforts.

Brokering social services

It was not intended that the Lak ICADP focus
solely on income generation (though the
communities’ fascination with cash, as a result of
their experience with logging, led this to be a
priority). The project had been designed to serve
as a broker of social services. The idea was to
negotiate the delivery of health and education
services to villagers as part of a conservation deal.
(This could be construed as one means of
compensating communities for the costs of
forgoing logging.) The government, by providing
a greater quantity and quality of services would, in
effect, be rewarding the Lak community for
supplying a public good.

The problem is that there is little political
support in PNG for such a scheme. Politicians are
far more concerned about delivering on electoral
promises to key electoral constituents. The Lak
community was too small to be politically
significant. A further problem was that there was
no precedent for such an approach. A more
compelling issue is that fiscal constraints were
leading the government to abandon and withdraw
services, and an increase in the quantity delivered to
Lak, for whatever reason, was out of the question.

towards development

Material incentives, for reasons already

come to reassess the Meaning of
development, the long-term consequences

conservation value of their forests. They

5.1.5 Cooperative endeavour
at the community level

If ICAD projects are to survive, they

require that recipient stakeholder

communities already have a high level of
social cohesion and cooperative endeavour.
Although the communities’ spirit of
cooperation can be strengthened through
targeted project activities, without an
inherent minimum level present during the
formative years, project survival is unlikely.

Conservation and sustainable development
initiatives require a high level of social, political
and economic cooperation if they are to be
successful. Conservation management usually
requires close consultation between a wide range
of resource owners to adequately facilitate the land
use planning and management systems necessary
for the establishment of formally protected areas.
This is particularly true in Papua New Guinea
where land areas of a size suitable for the
protection of biodiversity tend to include a very
large number of customary land parcels. Similarly,
many of the sustainable development initiatives—
such as eco-timber ventures—being suggested as
possibly suited to PNG’s rural areas, rely on inter-
clan and intra-clan partnerships to work. Where
inherent conflict exists between individuals or
within a clan or community, there is a grave risk to
the success of ICADPs.

This has serious implications for proponents
of ICADPs in PNG where traditional societies are
typically fragmented. Key individuals often act
against the local public good, owing to a lack of
common unifying ambitions and social control
mechanisms. Individual motives are inspired and
influenced by a diverse number of political
aspirations, personal greed, peer pressure, social
mores, customary obligations and community
structures. In dealings with outside players, these
individuals often claim to represent the body-
politic of the landowner community; they have
been remarkably successful in their pursuit of
personal benefits, even at the expense of
community welfare. Often, these “brokers” do not
have traditional forms of customary power,
[instead] having gained rank by positioning
themselves as experts with understanding of,
ability or experience in, a non-traditional field
(Toanusi pers. comm.).

Assessing social feasibility -

The Lak experiences reinforce the need for a
thorough site-specific social feasibility study.
ICAD projects must be fully informed on the
nature and complexities of social interaction
between individuals, clans and communities. If
these relationships are not favourable to the [CAD
approach, then the possibility of changing them
within an acceptable time frame must be assessed.
A question arises: Should ICAD projects build a
power base among bigmen—through providing
favours and “gifts” in accordance with the leaders’
status—before pursuing a more community-
oriented approach? This may be contrary to ICAD
philosophies and disagreeable to many
conservation proponents. Also, the changing
nature of leadership in traditional societies must
be taken into account. In the long term, project
stability may not be helped by building political

and economic links with current leaders.

Conservation workers need to establish
a broad network of partnership
arrangements with different stakeholders;

government and an international agency. It must
also be stated that PNG non-governmental
organisations vary widely in capacity, and those
organisations with a proven ability are usually
overcommitted. Many NGOs suffer from a lack of
resources, internal political instability, shortages in
technical capacity, and a fragmented
infrastructure.

The use of NGO skills in the Lak ICADP was
largely accomplished through Special Service
Agreements (SSAs), a contractual arrangement
whereby an organisation is paid to implement an
identified component of the overall project. From
the viewpoint of project management and
administration, this approach has several
advantages. Nevertheless, short-term involvement
of contractors in a project does little to encourage
commitment and dedication. A more serious
disadvantage is that short-term contractors lack a
stake in, and therefore ownership of, the project.
In the Lak ICADP, this manifested itself in
disappointing performances being returned by
some large PNG NGOs. This lesson has already
been applied to the Bismarck-Ramu ICADP,
where the Biodiversity Programme has made a
significant investment in support and
commitment to a local NGO from the earliest
stages of project design. It is now recognised that
NGOs that have an investment in project design
will be more committed to project philosophies,
activities and outcomes.

Communications between stakeholders

between stakeholders will have a large bearing on
the success of ICAD projects. In the Lak ICADP,

5.1.12 Conflicts between land use
management zonation and
customary land tenure systems

The land management requirements of

biodiversity protection mechanisms are

as yet incompatible with traditional land
tenure systems. Suitable interface
mechanisms are in the early stages of
development but are unlikely to offer
anything other than long-term solutions.

The Theory of Island Biogeography
(MacArthur & Wilson 1967), disputations aside,
holds that the number of species in recently
created islands (such as the rainforest pockets
created by human impacts) will decline to an
equilibrium level lower than that it was previously
holding. This is based on a fixed species—area
relationship. The smaller the habitat island, the
less biodiversity it contains. The implication is
that large land parcels will need to be allocated to
conservation management if species are to be
protected in the long term. This has serious
implications for conservation proponents in Papua
New Guinea where landowner groups tend to own
small parcels of land. Establishing a large
conservation area requires dealing with a large
number of ownership entities. In the case of Lak,
ownership of the 80,000 hectares of forest land
that make up the Timber Rights Purchase area is
divided between the 67 clans (landowning groups)
within the Siar language group. Establishing a
conservation compact with Lak communities

67 groups. When one considers the practical
implications of this, the extent and complexities of
joint land management systems become clearer.

Successful conservation initiatives, that are

to a buffer or transition zone, and a sustainable
development or controlled use zone. The problem

these needs with the realities of land tenure and
local social groupings.

5.1.14 Operational needs

The nature of the project’s operating

environment is not always fully

understood by project designers and
the senior management of parent agencies.
Operational difficulties are inevitable and

should be planned for.

While not all operational difficulties cam be
predicted accurately, many are inevitable “givens”
that can be expected to occur during the life of the
project. This is particularly important in
developing countries where the effects of
operational difficulties are often amplified because
the lack of infrastructure prevents the formulation
and adoption of contingency plans. Operational
difficulties experienced during the life of the Lak
ICADP included natural events such as the
eruption of the Rabaul volcanoes, several serious
earthquakes, and storms and resultant flooding.
Other constraints included attacks of malaria and
instances of localised civil unrest. The law and
order situation in PNG must also be considered a
constraint.

Operational constraints place a high
additional load on project resources. These
difficulties, even for a well-funded and well-
organised project, led to significant delays in
project implementation. The Lak experience leads
us to comment that the lines of communication
between field staff and senior management/
administration must be shortened and
streamlined. It is unrealistic to expect that a full
appreciation of the project's operating
environment will remain clear from project site,
to project office, to country office, and to head
office, particularly within short time frames.

5.1.16 Conservation initiatives are
disadvantaged in the present
political environment

The Lak ICAD project, drawing on

donor funds, was required to be

politically accountable, financially
transparent and culturally sensitive in its
operations. The logging contractor, on the
other hand, had few of these obligations
and enjoyed greater levels of flexibility
and autonomy. In the competition for the
forest allocation stakes, [CADPs—though
they might claim the moral high ground—
are inherently powerless because they have
to play by strict administrative rules.

As ICAD projects are focused on long-term
operating guidelines, they are required to establish
extensive networks of participation. This includes
both government and non-government
organisations, significant stakeholders and donor
bodies. A large investment of time and effort is
required to maintain these contacts so that
ICADPs are seen to be accountable and
responsible for their actions. Similarly, projects are
also required to work within restrictive financial
management guidelines, often at the expense of
flexibility and autonomy, to fulfil the obligations
of donor accountability.

ICAD projects should strive to be culturally
sensitive, both to the culture, traditions and beliefs
of the local stakeholder community, and to the
requirements of outside agencies (for example,
donors) who favour projects that undertake
exercises in community empowerment,
participation and gender equity. Furthermore,
project-sponsored development initiatives are
required to be both economically and
environmentally sustainable, typically needing
lengthy periods for adequate formulation, design
and planning.

All of these factors constrain ICADPs in
terms of available time, financial freedom and
operational dexterity. The same cannot be said for
many of the development agents operating in
direct competition with ICADPs for natural
resources. The “competition” for biodiversity
resources is, more often than not, stacked against

the ICADP from the outset.

Race for the Rainforest

The Lak experience

Lak provided many examples of opponents of the
ICADP not attempting to “play by the rules”.
Perhaps the worst example was that of the logging
contractor, in direct competition with the ICADP
for access to the Lak forest resource. While
harvesting the resource, the company continued
operations, despite being in breach of its
Environmental Plan. “The company has, in almost
every sense, blatantly flaunted the requirements of
the laws of Papua New Guinea, has not complied
. with as much as 80% of the Environmental Plan
approval conditions, and has totally disregarded
the provisions of the Environmental Planning Act
(1978)” (DEC 1995).

How the company continued to do this is
relatively clear, and relates to inefficiencies in the
policing of the industry, an extensive patron—client
relationship network reaching to the highest of
administrative levels, and developers who were
focused on maximising short-term benefits. In the
competition for Lak’s forest resources, there
seemed to be two separate sets of “rules”, with one
team playing fair, and the other playing to win.
Victory was inevitably assured to the latter.

Despite a letter of support for the Lak forestry
initiative from the Managing Director of the
National Forest Authority, the field officer of the
NFA in Lak actively campaigned against the
ICAD project. The officer in question allegedly
informed the Lak community that the ICAD
project was a loan, rather than a grant, that would
need to be repaid (Cooke pers. comm.). This,
though false, proved immensely damaging to the
ICAD project.

What are the lessons in this regard? Should
ICADPs play developers at their own game?
Would the establishment of patron—client
relationships, used so successfully by developers,
provide conservation proponents with the social
networks they require? Should the implementers
of projects concern themselves with political
correctness and administrative rules when the
stakes being fought for are so high?

Projects are compelled to behave “properly”
while they rely on public funds, operate within
indigenous communities, and plan for the long
term. However, it is clear that ICADPs can only
succeed in favourable environments. ICADPs
are often marginal prospects at best, in that they
face a huge number of barriers to their success.

ICADP are haunting, particularly when viewed as
the probable precursor to the outcomes of other
logging areas in PNG. Lak was perhaps one of the
more fortunate of rural communities, when it
captured the interest of an ICADP that was
prepared to spend vast amounts of finance, time
and energy pursuing sustainable development
initiatives. There are many other logging areas in
PNG, and very few have access to the technical
expertise necessary to harvest the resource wisely,
to minimise the environmental degradation and to
establish long-term sustainable development
investments. One has to ask, “Will they all end up
like Lak?” with a destroyed forest resource, far
fewer development options, few remaining
investments, and a lack of credibility in the eyes
of outside agencies.

To reiterate the comments of the Project
Review Mission: “In the event that the Lak
ICADP is scaled down or abandoned, it is
important that it be seen as an experience, a trial,
and not as a failure. The knowledge thus
accumulated will be of great value not only for the
development of the second ICAD site and other
ICAD initiatives in Papua New Guinea but also
on a broader international scale”? (UNDP 1995).
The Lak ICADP failed in its primary objective but
has yielded many valuable lessons that are being
used in the Bismarck-Ramu ICADP and are now
available for future project planners. This is a
widespread trend for ICADPs, as Brown and
Wyckoff (1992) write: “[ICADPs] are actually
experiments using new methodologies in
conservation and sustainable development. As
such, they are not based on a body of tested
knowledge, but rather are the building blocks
of theory and future efforts” (p. xiii).

It is the hope of the authors, and the
contributors to the PNG Biodiversity Programme,
that the Lak project has made a significant
contribution to the evolution of integrated
conservation and development mechanisms and
successful methodologies, which will lead to the
formation of a protected area network that,
simultaneously, both meets the sustainable
development needs of local resource owners and
protects Papua New Guinea's invaluable
biodiversity endowment.
