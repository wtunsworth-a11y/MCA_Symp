Foreword

The Biodiversity Conservation and Resource
Management Programme (BCRMP) in Papua
New Guinea, run jointly by the United Nations
Development Programme and the Government of
Papua New Guinea, was funded by the Global
Environment Facility (GEF). It aimed to meet the
global need for conservation of wilderness areas
around the world by supporting national and local
needs, in capacity-building and for development.

One aspect of the work carried out by the
BCRMP was the establishment of two Integrated
Conservation and Development (ICAD) projects.
It was intended from the start that these would be
innovative, trialling new methodologies in the race
to ensure that pristine terrestrial and marine
environments were set aside for conservation
before they were exploited and destroyed.

In 1997 the BCRMP published a book, Race
for the Rainforest, which told the story of the first
of these innovative experiments in ICAD work,
carried out at Lak, in southern New Ireland. This
experiment did not lead to the hoped-for result of
increased land formally set aside for conservation
in Papua New Guinea, and the Lak project was
terminated in 1996.

However, rather than simply let the project fall
into oblivion, the BCRMP then took the
courageous step of publishing the details of the
experiment and thus putting on the public record
what went wrong, and why, and most importantly,
what lessons could be drawn from the experiment,
for the benefit of future CAD work in Papua
New Guinea. These ‘lessons from Lak’ formed an
important part of the Race for the Rainforest, and
have been widely read and applauded.

This book, Race for the Rainforest 2, tells the
further story of how those lessons were applied to
the second ICAD project undertaken by the
BCRMP. and how, as a result, over 120,000
hectares of land to date have been designated by
the traditional owners for special conservation
status. The Bismarck-Ramu project, profiting
from the earlier project, and from the intense re-
evaluation and reconsideration which followed it,
established a pace-setting participatory
methodology. This was coupled with a
determination that for the Bismarck-Ramu the
‘Development’, the “D’ in ICAD, would be
genuine community development, empowering
the communities to work towards their own goals,
not simply an externally-devised notion of
economic development.

The United Nations Development Programme
in Papua New Guinea is proud to be associated
with the work of the Bismarck-Ramu project and
with this book, as one more in the valuable series
of publications issuing from the BCRMP, now
being read and used by those actively engaged in
sustainable development initiatives in Papua New

Guinea.

1.1 Papua New Guinea's
Environment

Ecologically, Papua New Guinea is one of
the world’s most exciting countries, with
‘immeasurably species rich forests, wetlands, and
coastal marine systems.’! It ‘probably harbours
more than five percent of the world’s biodiversity

within some of the world’s most biologically
2

The richness of PNG’s biodiversity is a reserve,
an asset, both for the population of PNG itself,
and for the world. Most immediately, for PNG, it
is the source of the nation’s livelihood. Some 85%
of Papua New Guineans live in rural areas, and of
these most rely directly on the environment for
their livelihood. (Land use rights, the single
greatest determinant of well-being, are close to
universal.) The environment produces most
obviously food, through subsistence gardening,
hunting and fishing; it also produces medicine,
building materials, fibre for various uses and
cultural goods, such as the highly valued bird-of-
paradise plumage, which is still part of the
working internal economy of the country.

The immense productivity of hunting, fishing,
subsistence gardening, and cultural production is
almost all in the informal sector, and so is often
undervalued by first-world-based economists.?
Moreover, in the context of PNG’s development
focus, the ‘invisibility’ of this productivity in
conventional economic terms has contributed to
a political weakness in the environmental lobby.

The ‘invisible’ productivity of the primary forest,
for example, is undervalued compared to the
potential for the visible productivity of large-scale
logging. Nevertheless the dependence of the bulk
of Papua New Guinea’s people on a continuing
productivity from the natural environment, is very
great indeed, and the national loss which could be
sustained through damage to the environment is
correspondingly high.

Within PNG, at least one preliminary study
has been done to lay a foundation for assessing the
subsistence value of the biodiversity.* This
suggested, in 1994, a figure for subsistence
agriculture production value of between K600
million and K3000 million per year.> However,
in the absence of recent and detailed information
this could only be an estimate; the study
concluded that precise valuation was not possible
at this point, but that the subsistence value of
biodiversity was ‘a major component in the
nation’s economy... [which] underpins the welfare
of the majority of Papua New Guinea's rural
populace, and by all accounts will continue to do
so in the future.”

It is more straightforward to estimate the
substantial actual and potential value of marketed
goods taken directly from the wildlands of Papua
New Guinea — that is to say, dependent in the
long term on the conservation of the environment
— although here, too, lack of recent quantitative
data, and the possibility of understatement of |
those goods attracting an export tax, such as
crocodile skins, has proved a constraint on exact

1.2 Conservation and PNG

Yet this rich environment, a resource for
the world as well as for its own people, is under
pressure. If it is seen as an asset by its own people,
and by the world’s environmental lobby, it is also
seen as an exploitable asset by powerful logging,
mining and fishing interests. Internal exploitation
of resources is also a source of concern, as growing
populations, combined with devastating new
methods of hunting and fishing — as for example,
dynamite-fishing and indiscriminate poisoning —
stress the resources available. In some areas, long-
standing population density has already led to
diminished biodiversity, as in large areas of the
highlands, where great expanses of kunai grass
have replaced the former complex biosystem of the
primary forest. Simultaneously, smuggling of
species from the country is rife, and it is not
inconceivable that Papua New Guinea may itself
lose access to valuable resources just as they are
being exploited elsewhere.

Papua New Guinea in its Constitution
declared as a fundamental goal of the nation that
the country’s ‘natural resources and

environment... be conserved and used for the
collective benefit of us all, and... be replenished
for the benefit of future generations’. While
naturally the commitment of individual politicians
and governments to this ideal varies, recent
governments have made PNG a signatory to
various international conventions and treaties
relating to the environment, such as the
Convention on International Trade in Endangered
Species (CITES), the International Plant
Protection Convention, and, perhaps most
significantly, the Convention on Biological
Diversity. This last provides for signatory
countries, amongst other responsibilities, to
establish a system of protected areas to conserve
biodiversity resources, and to promote environ-
mentally sound and sustainable development for
communities living adjacent to conservation areas,
in order to further protection for those areas.

However, the machinery of government in
Papua New Guinea is not strong. More especially,
since land is owned overwhelmingly by traditional
owners, in customary clan-based holdings, the
state is not in a strong position to enforce either
the constitutional ideal or the national obligations
under international conventions.®

Less than 2.8 per cent of Papua New Guinea
has formal protected area status. Those formally
protected areas which do exist do not go near to
covering the country’s astonishingly diverse
habitats. The land area set aside is in any case not
great, and in many cases the reserved area is ‘too
small to adequately protect biodiversity’. Of the
land which is set aside, only just over 1% is state-
owned National Park land; by far the bulk of the
protected land in Papua New Guinea is
traditionally owned, protected not directly by the
national government, but locally, under the Fauna
(Protection and Control) Act. This allows
landowners to establish their own committees to
set and enforce locally binding regulations for the
protection of wildlife, giving the backing of the
national government to local management.
However, only nominal penalties apply, and local

However, in terms of the possibilities for
implementing conservation, Papua New Guinea
has an extraordinary potential. That customary
landownership has been a strength for Papua New
Guinea, in terms of conservation as well as of
general livelihood, is clear. Customary ownership
has acted to limit clearance for settlement, in
colonial times, and for plantations, both in
colonial times and more recently; the
extraordinary complexity of the landholding
system even acts currently as a mild deterrent to
some ‘development’. Further, the passionate
commitment of the people to the traditional
landholding system, and the existence of a strong
subsistence economy as a support system, allows
optimal conditions for the development of small-
scale integrated conservation and development
projects.

1.3 Integrated Conservation
and Development in PNG

The idea of integrating conservation and
development sprang from two realisations. One
was that conservation by exclusion of
neighbouring communities from wildland areas

Part 1: Background to the Bismarck-Ramu ICAD Project

was fundamentally an injustice, given that such
communities frequently depended on the
environmental resource for their livelihood. The
second was that this injustice led in turn to
instability in the administration of conservation
areas, as normal hunting or wood-gathering, for
example, having been defined as illegal by
conservation enactments, retreated from the
possibility of surveillance and management.

Concurrently, there was the belief that
landowners of unexploited lands should be
compensated for agreeing, in the global interest, to
leave their lands as biodiversity reserves. As the
then Prime Minister of PNG put it in 1990:

If this [forest] resource is considered of such high
ecological value to the nation or to the world
community that it should be fully conserved, then
the villagers should be compensated for the

opportunities foregone by not harvesting it. !°

International attention turned, therefore, to
the provision of economic and social development
for traditional landowners, neighbouring or
resident communities, as a method of support for
conservation. Projects so directed came to be
known as Integrated Conservation and
Development Projects, or ICADs.!! There is no
one model for ICAD work, and much of the work
is still in a sense experimental, ‘not based on a
body of tested knowledge, but rather... the
building blocks of theory and future efforts’. 14
Some projects have attempted to work through the
direct provision of social development projects,
such as literacy education or health service
establishment. In some cases, the emphasis has
been on infrastructure development, such as water
supply systems. Frequently the emphasis has been
on economic development, or even direct income
provision.

Given the nation-wide traditional ownership
of land, and the dependence of the bulk of the
population on the environment for subsistence
living, conservation by exclusion of human use
was not and is not a possibility in Papua New
Guinea. If the environment is to be conserved,
therefore, it must be done, at least in part, through
the ICAD approach. This was explicitly recognised

Moreover, the strong ‘user pays’ imperative in
PNG, where rural communities, for example,
must provide their own health and education
infrastructure, makes the search for ‘development
an immediate threat to the environment, since
landowners see the opportunity cost in foregoing,
for example, logging, reflected in a lack of essential
services. Conversely, ‘developers’ (most often
logging and mining companies) offer provision of
such services as part of the price to be paid for the
right to exploit traditionally-owned land. If
conservation was to happen, it must happen in an
ICAD context. As environments are under
immediate threat ICADs must either establish
sustainable development of resources, in
contradistinction to the unsustainable use offered
by industrial logging, or, where sustainable
development is not able to meet the opportunity
cost of foregoing ‘development’, the ICAD must
also provide additional goods and services.

Eight such projects are already in existence in
PNG. These range from the coastal-area Maisin
ICAD, where cottage-industry production of
traditional tapa-cloth has been managed to
provide good export income, to the highlands
Crater Mountain ICAD, where direct servicing of
science-related travel, through provision of guides,
accommodation and trained auxiliary staff allows
communities to appreciate the direct economic
benefit of a pristine environment.!4 Several of
these have been assisted by international non-
government conservation organisations.

1.4 The Biodiversity
Conservation and Resource
Management Programme
The potential of ICAD projects in Papua

New Guinea was recognised by the Global

Environment Facility (GEF), a body set up in
1990 to administer funds for projects with global

environmental benefits, and jointly administered
by the United Nations Development Programme
(UNDP), the World Bank and the United Nations
Environment Programme (UNEP).

In 1993 the UNDP-GEF funded the PNG
Biodiversity Conservation and Resource
Management Programme (BCRMP), locally
managed by the national Department of
Environment and Conservation, as one part of the
broader PNG National Forestry and Conservation
Action Programme (NFCAP), which was itself a
response to the World Bank’s Tropical Forest
Action Plan (TFAP).

The principal aim of the BCRMP is ‘to
expand the country’s protected area system in
order to establish a representative system that
provides for the management of PNG’s
considerable biodiversity endowment.’!? More
immediately, the BCRMP had three objectives:
¢ to establish pilot ICADs, developing

innovative methodologies for the conservation

of biodiversity;

* to provide institutional strengthening to the
Department of the Environment and
Conservation, for the conservation of
biodiversity; and

° to establish an institutional, legal, financial
and policy framework for the expansion and
future maintenance of the conservation
system.

One of the two areas first identified as targets
for the pilot ICAD programme was the subject
area of this study, the Bismarck-Ramu. The other
was the Lak area of New Ireland, where the
progress and outcome of the ICAD experiment
were to have far-reaching effects on the design of
the Bismarck-Ramu project.

1.5 The Lak ICAD Project

The Lak ICAD site was on the large island
of New Ireland, running up the valley of the
Weitin River from the coast. The biodiversity
value was clear; the area had been included in a list

The ICAD at Lak, therefore, was the first
ICAD project set up under the BCRMP in PNG,
in response to what was seen as an imminent
threat to an inestimable resource. Since
landowners were attracted to logging and other
forms of exploitation by the promise of direct
financial benefit, it was decided to work by
offering equivalent direct benefit, to demonstrate
to community landholders how the land could be
a money-spinning resource. Because logging had
already begun, it was seen as important that the
direct benefits be offered as soon as possible, and
the approach became established as offering ‘early
rewards’, tangible benefits which could be opposed
to the ‘benefits’ offered through logging.

The BCRMP team moved in and established a
variety of actions with immediate benefit for the
community. A twenty-year lease was taken over a
block of land at Leanis, close to the Weitin Valley.
This was cleared, and eight buildings erected. The
buildings used Melanesian design features, and
were solidly constructed, making them a visible

sign of the ICAD commitment to the area and to
Papua New Guinean culture. The establishment of
the base provided immediate returns to the
community in the form of money for rent of the
block, of payment for materials and labour, and of
the provision of on-the-job training for
community members in building techniques and
cross-cultural design.

The ICAD team also set about initiating a
landowner awareness campaign. For this, they

innovative methodologies, had not yielded the
desired result. The Lak ‘experiment’ was
terminated.

Strictly speaking, no experiment can be a
‘failure’, since the proper outcome of an
experiment is the observation and analysis of
results and refinement of method, which are
always available, no matter how far the result
might be from that expected or desired. The
experimenters of Lak were not detached from their
experiment — they were fervently committed to
the conservation of the biodiversity. They hoped,
passionately, for a particular result — the cessation
of logging and the establishment of a conservation
area at Lak. But when this did not result, they did
not simply abandon the experiment; instead they
moved to the natural next phase, the analysis of
results and the refining of the ICAD method, for
further use in Papua New Guinea and elsewhere.
The BCRMP adopted as a revised project output
the publicising of the lessons learned from Lak,
and the book consequently written by two of the
BCRMP staff, Race for the Rainforest: evaluating
lessons from an Integrated Conservation and
Development “experiment” in New Ireland, Papua

New Guinea, has been widely read, and of great
benefit. It is also of some importance for this
current study, since the last section in that book
details the ‘lessons learned’ from Lak — lessons
which have shaped the whole of the Bismarck-
Ramu project since early 1996.

1.6 The Lessons Learned
McCallum and Sekhran identified

seventeen different ‘lessons’, identified below by
the numbering given in their 1997 survey of the
project, Race for the Rainforest.2? In summary
these fall into four areas, relating to the selection
of the site for the ICAD, the method of
community entry into the area, the provision of
a supportive and informed working environment,
and the actual direction of activities at the site.

In the selection of a site for an ICAD the
assessment of social, political, economic and
institutional factors was seen to be essential, as
well as biological criteria. The selection of Lak,
where logging was already established and
supported by most of the community was felt in
retrospect to be almost setting up the ICAD to fail
— the odds were simply too strong. The logging
company had ‘enormous political bargaining
power at the national level and had established a
robust local power base’.24_ Moreover, as a private
agent without direct accountability, the company
was able to use tactics which, to say the least,
skirted the margins of legality and morality. The
recommendation for the future methodology of
ICADs in PNG was that site selection should not
be swayed by the urgency of the threat, nor by a
enthusiastic desire to ‘challenge’ developers to a
‘duel’.?> Instead, after biological criteria have
suggested several possible sites, ‘socio-economic
criteria must dictate the actual choice of site’.
(Lesson 5.1.1) This was to include assessment of
what might be called macro-factors, such as the
politics and institutions involved in the area

2.1 The Bismarck-Ramu Area

Location and landforms

“When God created the world he was
caught out by nightfall and not able to finish
the job. As a result, the Upper Jimi Valley,
one of the last areas to be finished, ended up
as a jumble of mountains and rocks thrown

about.’26

The Bismarck-Ramu Area of Interest is in
the heart of Papua New Guinea, sloping down
from the central highlands of the country towards
the northern coast. It plunges from the sharply
jagged peaks of the Bismarck Range down through
extensive primary forests to the flood plains of the
Ramu River, dropping from the highest point in
PNG, at some 4500 metres, to a mere sixty metres
above sea level.

Broadly speaking, it is a kite-shaped area, with
its southernmost point in the vicinity of the jagged
peak of Mt Wilhelm — PNG’s highest mountain
— in Simbu Province. From that point, its
boundaries spread north-west and north-east, on
either side of Mt Wilhelm. Running north-west,
the boundary roughly follows the line of the
jagged Bismarck Range, but including the Jimi

River Valley south of that Range, in the Western
Highlands Province. The boundary running
north-east drops steeply down, finally running
along the Marum River to the Ramu River, in
Madang province. The Ramu and Simbai Rivers
form the remaining boundaries, with the
northernmost corner the junction of the two, in

Madang province. The area thus spreads into three
provinces. It also covers very diverse habitats,
which may be seen as falling into three broad
divisions: the Jimi Valley, the Bismarck Fall area
and the Ramu River plain.

Both the Middle Jimi Valley and the Upper
Jimi are in mountainous country. The Upper Jimi,
especially, is “hardly recognisable as a valley, due to
the deeply incised rivers which are separated by
high interfluves.’*”. These mountain ridges are
the site of most of the settlements and villages of
Upper Jimi. The Jimi itself originates near Mt.
Wilhelm, but is fed by numerous smaller rivers; it
is bounded on the southern side by the Sepik-
Waghi Divide, and on the other by the Bismarck
Range. The soil throughout the Jimi is shallow
and nutrient-deficient. There is a marked wet
season from November to March and a dry season
from May to August, and a daily temperature
variation leading to frost risk, limiting the
agricultural potential of the area. Above all, it is
sharply rugged country, with level ground a rarity.
Nevertheless, it is densely populated, and this has
impacted on both vegetation and wildlife. Near
settlements, especially, any forest is secondary
growth, and there is little wildlife remaining for
hunting.

The Ramu Valley, by contrast, is hot and
humid lowland, the floodplain of the mighty
Ramu, with all its loops and billabongs. The river
is especially menacing in wet season, when debris
rides the high water brought by the many streams
running down from the Bismarck, Adelbert and
Finisterre Ranges, and the flood spreads for several
kilometres across the plain. Though its path seems

Between the Jimi and the Ramu River area is
the steep declivity of the Bismarck Fall, an area
with very few permanent settlements, ‘virtually
uninhabited’2? but used for hunting and other
purposes by both the Jimi and the Ramu people.
This area, a region of high biodiversity, partly
protected from immediate threat of logging and
mining by difficulty of access, lies at the core of
the Area of Interest. The value of the natural
heritage in this area, and the importance of its
relatively unpopulated state, has long been
recognised, and has led to a series of attempts to
ensure its conservation.

As early as 1969, in the closing years of
Australia’s administration of Papua New Guinea,
part of the current Area of Interest, was identified
as a Proposed National Park Area, to be called the
Simbai National Park, or Ramu National Park.
The proposal reached the stage of tentative
boundary-setting, and of agreement from
landowners, the Madang Area government and the
then National Park Service. However it was never
gazetted and in 1986 the proposal was shelved,
chiefly due to increasing settlement in the area.
In 1981, a smaller national park was proposed,
surrounding the peak of Mt Wilhelm; the PNG
government purchased from the traditional
owners, some 810 hectares around the peak of Mt
Wilhelm, and confirmed the intention to declare a
National Park in the Government Gazette of May
1990. In the event, the formal declaration and
gazettal have been postponed pending resolution
of a dispute between the government and the
traditional owners.

closest of these are Ukul and Kukupang. Besides the
local landowners, there is a substantial influx of
settlers from the Jimi region in all the surrounding
villages. Pimbum itself formerly had a larger population,
which scattered when fighting between landowners
and Jimi settlers led to many deaths some years back.

In 1991 an airstrip was opened at Foroko, after
initial consideration had been given to opening one at
Pimbum. Shortly after, local government boundaries
were changed, leading Pimbum communities to feel
cut off from the services at Foroko. They then decided
that the best way forward was to build an airstrip at
Pimbum. In July 1994 John Opo Akis, a geologist from
the Jimi area, but with extensive family connections in
the Ramu valley, responded to requests from his
community to build a house at Pimbum, in
anticipation of, and as an aid to, future growth of
Pimbum as a service centre.*’ Opo’s family also put
forward his name as their spokesman after the April
1995 patrol; he was employed initially as field

and thereafter given a year’s contract from January
1996 as Bismarck-Ramu Community Facilitator.

In September 1995, when a BCRMP team walked down
to Pimbum as part of the preparation for the following
month's Biological Survey, the communities raised one
matter directly: they needed support and help with
development for the airstrip. The visiting team
responded immediately, offering help to find money
and otherwise help with the construction of the

In January 1996 BCRMP made good on that vague

In February 1996, the Conservation Area Manager and

back-up for the ICAD patrols. Hostilities began to rise
at Foroko, which at that time was engaged in a
compensation dispute with Pimbum. However, the
Foroko community allowed the patrol to leave with the
radio equipment. This was duly installed at the house
belonging to John Opo, and two local Jimi settlers
were employed to look after the radio and the house.
This was seen by nearby residents as a confirmation of
John Opo’s pivotal role in the ICAD, and also as a sign
that the ICAD ‘base’ would be at Pimbum.

2.3 The termination of
the Lak project

In the meantime, all had not gone well at
Lak. The project had certainly come close to
success, a remarkable achievement given the odds
against it. The benefits to the community from the
ICAD project were beginning to be seen, and the
benefits from logging were falling. The
community were apparently being won over to the
idea of a cessation of large-scale logging and the
establishment of a conservation zone. In October
1995, a community forum attended by 250
community members had debated the future of

logging in the area, agreeing overwhelmingly to
abandon large-scale logging, as had also been
agreed at similar meetings in October 1994 and
April 1995. However, the difficulty came in the
implementation of this decision — community
representatives on the board of the Metlak
Development Corporation, after very intense
lobbying by the timber interests concerned, acted
to ensure continued logging.

Nor were the community prepared to
challenge the board in this matter. Their
reluctance, which may have had several causes, was

be that the community looked to the staff to take

commitment. The Lak ICAD Project was formally
terminated on 30 August 1996.

2.4 The ICAD project after Lak

As indicated above, the BCRMP group
were throughout 1995 considering alternative
methodologies to the Lak approach. Even if Lak
had been a success, the brief to develop innovative

constant reassessment of the BCRMP

investigation, learning and reassessment.

Melanesian Institute, the Liklik Dinau Scheme?!

elements of the NSRDP were to prove particularly

Most fundamentally for the design and

the economic development emphasis of Lak.
Instead, the BCRMP began to take seriously the
notion, often given lip-service in international
development work, that real development lies in
empowering the community to undertake its own
development, and conversely, that top-down and
non-participatory provision of infrastructure,
material goods and services, however attractive to
the community, can be in fact disempowering, and
thus negative in its effects.

Internationally [CAD work has been founded
on the notion that conservation of biodiversity is
primarily an opportunity cost, in terms of
socioeconomic benefits. It has therefore
concentrated on the socioeconomic benefits to be
achieved through a direct linkage to conservation.
Acceptance of conservation has been the presented
as the key to start and maintain economic
development, to offset the supposed opportunity
cost of foregoing resource exploitation. The
forming of linkages between the economic
development and conservation had therefore been
regarded as essential to ICAD work: ‘all material
benefits should be clearly linked to conservation
action.’°4 Conservation action was presented as a

motivator, prompting outside agencies or clients to

deliver economic development.

In a fundamental shift, to meet the PNG
context as re-evaluated after Lak, the BCRMP
decided that: ‘the ICAD community liaison staff
must start by not talking about conservation, not
talking about economic incentives, and not talking
about development... Instead they should be
listening to the community, and facilitating a
debate within the community as it explores its
own needs and beliefs... Conservation and the
establishment of protected areas will be the
outcomes, not the motivators.’”°

This subordination of economic development
to community development was the first
formulation of what was later to be more closely
defined (see Section 3.1, below) as the major
working hypothesis of the Bismarck-Ramu
project. This is that:

° — since CAD agencies do not have the resources
to ‘outbid’ resource exploiters in the provision
of goods and services; and

° since, in the PNG context, with strong
subsistence livelihoods directly provided from
the environment, any long-term analysis (and
‘long-term’ here can mean a period as short as
twenty years) will show that conservation is
not an opportunity cost, but the preservation
of a continuously productive asset-base; and

© — since the only land realistically available for
conservation is traditionally-owned
community land,

° therefore genuine community conviction about
the need for conservation is the only thing
which will defend the resources;

¢ and the development of such conservation
conviction will only come about as a result of
community development and planning
process, when people choose ‘with their eyes
open to conserve the biodiversity of their land
resource.

3.1 Reworking the
Project Design

The overall objective of the BCRMP
remained as first formulated, ‘to expand the
conservation system of Papua New Guinea to
provide for biodiversity conservation’. The
immediate objective of the ICAD component of
the programme was ‘to establish pilot ICADs to
develop innovative methodologies for conservation
of biodiversity’.°® The intention in the Bismarck-
Ramu area was still to see a section of land, large
enough to be effective in conservation of
biodiversity, set aside under Papua New Guinea's
Conservation Areas Act.

However, the ‘lessons from Lak’ had made
plain the existence of flaws in the original project
design, for the Papua New Guinean context, as
well as in implementation. While the Project
Document had ‘envisaged that a range of benefits
will be provided to landowners on an ongoing
basis, in return for development rights foregone’,
the Lak trial had indicated that this was not
entirely appropriate for PNG, a matter discussed

at the September Workshop and in the Framework
Plan,

A major issue is that economic incentives are
relatively weak motivators in Melanesian society.
Rural Melanesians are much less marginalised (in a
welfare sense) than societies in many developing
countries. In marginalised societies, people will put
out a lot of energy and effort for relatively little
reward. In less marginalised societies, people are
less compelled to expend work effort to meet given

Plan to building and implementing
a process of planning

objectives. As a result, in PNG, incentives are
generally attractive only if relatively small amounts
of energy are required to gain relatively large
rewards. In short, the kinds of incentives which are
attractive are those that require little sweat equity
in return. The distinction between this form of
incentives and handouts is blurred. Mountains of
evidence point to the fact that handouts are not
the way to stimulate sustainable initiatives, unless
somehow the handouts themselves can be
guaranteed in perpetuity. And sustained or not,
handouts don’t build motivation.

Especially, the economic incentives approach
was judged to be ineffective unless it was preceded
by the building of conservation conviction, rather
than just awareness, as called for in the Project
Document. Further, since in Papua New
Guinea, clan-based landholdings are too small to
be of real significance as biodiversity conservation
areas, the process of negotiation with many clans
to set aside all their landholdings simultaneously
into a large conservation area was simply too
complex for achievement to be feasible in the five-
year timeframe given.

The project documentation, however, had
foreseen that the ‘innovative and experimental
character’ of the BCRMP made redesign,
especially of the ICAD process and after the
midterm review, a probable option during the
project period.°! Throughout 1996, then, the
BCRMP team was engaged in a process of intense
debate, reconsideration and redesign. This process
in itself was strongly participatory, drawing on the

Subsequent recruitment and training

Subsequent CDT recruitment has also been made
through personal approach, especially at the
recommendation of existing CDTs, thus ensuring
that the teams are cohesive. As well, it was part of
the project design that there should be as far as
possible a gender balance in the CDTs, and
recruitment especially aimed to keep
approximately equal numbers of men and women,
building on the original team, where two of five
members were women. Addressing this issue of
balance is especially important (and perhaps
especially difficult) in PNG, where a long
traditional culture of male dominance has been
reinforced by a colonial period where the powerful
kiaps (government officers) in rural areas were all
male.

The recruits join existing CDTs for training,
along the lines of the training outlined above, and
also for the briefings before a patrol, although they

do not go on patrol until further assessment as to
whether they will work well with the existing
teams.

The CDT training programme from 1996 to
1998 has resulted in the production of two
training manuals, suitable for use in similar ICAD
training, especially in PNG and the nearby Pacific
region.

As well as ongoing training in community
development work, there is a constant re-
evaluation of the direction and methodologies of
the programme, through an intensive process of
preparation for and review of each patrol. Before
each patrol, all the CDTs meet, along with the
trainer and other staff as appropriate, to work
through the preparation for the coming trip, for
example, by role-plays and assessment of materials
available. After patrols, debriefing sessions analyse
the achievements and the difficulties of the teams,
noting even details such as body language used in
meetings, as well as unexpected difficulties, and
possible changes of direction. As an example, after
one patrol the CDTs and the Madang-based staff
together worked through the matter of food
provided voluntarily by communities to CDTs.
The matter was discussed from several angles,
whether it might be better to pay for the food, to
block any potential accusation of CDTs using up
scarce community resources, or whether this might
be disempowering, since gift-giving, especially in

respect for the recipient.

3.3 The Timeframe

It was understood from the start that this
could not be a quick process. The seven-step
process could not be rushed, and there were
misconceptions to be addressed even before work
could really begin. This was stressed by the
midterm review mission, and was a direct response
to the problems of Lak, where acceptance of an
unrealistic timeframe (in part imposed by the
immediacy of the logging threat) contributed to
the project’s difficulties.

The March 1996 version of the Framework
Plan assessed that “Declaration of protected areas is

turned out, this prediction was almost exactly on

community development process began, the first

3.5 The Social Feasibility Study

The other major contributor to this
process of ongoing project design was the
gathering of historical and anthropological
material as a basis for a social analysis, known
within the project as the Social Feasibility Study
(SFS). The foundations for this had been laid in
April 1995, with the visit to the area of the
Director of the National Research Institute (the
body contracted to carry out the study) who had
also mapped out a literature search.

But the bulk of the study was done on site,
along with the first stage of community entry and
the community development process. The
expatriate researcher agreed to wait until the CDTs
had entered an area, and had become acquainted

Part 3: From implementing a Blue-print Plan to building and implementing a process of planning

with the community, before being introduced by
them, to carry out his researches. This was a
controversial decision, since a ‘feasibility study’
would more normally be carried out before entry.
However, on balance, and after wide consultation,
the BCRMP team decided that the presence of
any expatriate researcher might compromise the
credibility of the CDTs, and hamper their work.
This was possible in two ways. Memories of
colonial times meant that sometimes the presence
of an expatriate on patrol was felt by village
communities to increase the credibility of patrols,
but it also tended to raise expectations about the
results of the patrol, leading to disappointment
and anger if no cargo was forthcoming. More
importantly, unless elaborate disclaimers were
made, the ‘whiteskin’ present was invariably taken
to be the leader of the patrol, eroding the
credibility of the Papuan New Guinean workers.

For these reasons, the social and
anthropological data was gathered during the first
stages of the community development process;
thereafter the two aspects of the work ran in
parallel, rather than sequentially. This was not an
ideal solution; it meant, obviously, that social
analysis could only follow entry by the CDTs; it
also meant that communities not entered by the
CDTs during the period of employment of the
researcher were not covered by the SFS, or covered
only through the literature study already
conducted. A thorough social analysis before entry
might have helped the CDT to predict and avoid
some confusions. However, the researcher himself
felt that the benefits of the parallel working
arrangement outweighed the disadvantages,
concluding that “it is clear that without the close
link with the community development teams
much less information could and would have been
collected than is presently the case.’ 7!

As well, and most significantly for the
development of the project, working in parallel
allowed the researcher to become part of the CDT
team discussions, feeding directly into the work as
it progressed, and thus contributing to the
constant reworking of approach and methodology.
This also put into practice a fundamental principle
of the study: social research, often carried out on
Papua New Guineans for the benefit of others, was
to be returned to the informants, through its

4.1 First entry, and
the praxis approach

In mid-July 1996, the initial training was
finished, and it was time for the Community
Development Teams to move into action. Two
teams went out, one to the Jimi Valley area, and
one to the Ramu River country. The plan for this
initial trip was to visit a few selected villages, first
to stay in each village for two or three days, to
meet village leaders and generally get to know the
community. It was planned that a few days later
the teams would return to each community to
hold formal meetings, using the timeline and the
village mapping exercise, and finding out the
issues of importance to the community.

And straight away the whole future method of
operation of the Bismarck-Ramu ICAD project

fell into place. This time, unlike the main thrust of

the Lak ICAD, there was not to be one grand
‘experiment’, a settled land-use project at a pre-
determined site, to which clans had to be won
over. Rather, there would be an ongoing process of
praxis, the action-reflection-action which
constantly reassesses methodologies and refashions
approaches to fit the demands of actuality. The
mechanics of this process had already been
established in the briefing-patrol-debriefing
system, but the living reality of praxis as a way of
working came with the first entry into the field.
Each team, as a response to the field conditions,
developed a second mechanism of continual
reassessment; team members informally assessed
each other, after meetings or more casual CD
work, reinforcing strengths or suggesting changes
in word use, body language, tone and so on.

The PRA tools which had been worked through in
training were taken into the villages — they
proved, in the context of the leisurely discussions
that the CDTs were able to have, very powerful.
For example, at Nolumb village, the resource
mapping tool proved to be immensely involving
and stimulating, sparking consideration of the
causes of environmental degradation, and even of
the reasons for, and likely effects of, the Jimi
migration into the Ramu area. (See Box: Resource

such as at Ambisiba, in March 1998, where “They

changes have occured in our own community.”

empty circle next to the two drawn maps. He said,

The semi-structured interview, when used in
conjunction with ‘storying’, also proved to be a
useful tool. Thus, at Miale village:

The example of bride price payment was used
effectively to convey that community organising,
planning and self-reliance are not new. The men
were challenged to relate the entire process of bride

price payment as it was and is practised at Miale.
This was real interesting stuff, because practically
every man present (youngsters included) naturally
contributed to the discussion. As is normal in this
area, the initiative of bride price payment lies in
the hands of the groom and his immediate family.
The extended family, other relatives and friends
from all over offer their contributions only after
the groom and his immediate family had taken
some initiative. Through semi-structured
interviewing and through probing, the men were
led to some understanding that if they want to get
anything at all done in their village or area, they
must do it themselves, or at least take some
initiative to do something first.

However, CDTs were careful not to rely too
much on these tools at the expense of the simple
tools of community entry. They reflected at an
early debriefing ‘that PRA tools, powerful as they
are, tend to deflect attention from storying and
trust-building exercise. We should avoid short-
cutting our introductions to get on with the
gimmick. PRA tools can be used to focus people's
attention, to tease out linkages... but one should
avoid reducing the community entry process to
PRA only,’8°

solutions to community problems, but rather to

especially, the most frequently repeated
watchword, ‘isi-isi’, the slow and steady approach
which allowed the work time to ripen, not
hurrying to meet an external schedule.

4.3 Constraints on CD work

There have of course been constraints and
difficulties affecting the CD work, including, in
1997, the unusual stress of elections and severe
drought. Apart from these, the constraints with
greatest impact on CDT work were the danger of
harassment by criminals and the simple hardship
of travel in a rugged, unserviced region.

Harassment by criminals

The PNG bush area is not necessarily ‘safe’ for
visitors; the CDTs experienced the usual dangers
and difficulties of travel in a place where police
services are almost unknown, where clan loyalties
usually protect criminals and where the bush
provides an almost impenetrable hiding place. The

saved our necks and managed to ‘outclass’ their
strategies, mainly because we never disclosed
definite plans to the public about our movements.

An added constraint is that the gang rape of
women, especially women outside the clan or
allied groups, is unhappily not infrequent in PNG.
In a time when group allegiances are being tested
and contested, such as in the 1997 election period,
the risk of violence, including rape, is greatly
heightened. The CDTs, with the full support of
the BCRMP, decided that the worker safety issue

gender equity needs of the project, and during the
high tension time of the election women CDT
members did not go on foot patrol in the Upper
Jimi. This tense time has now passed, and women
are again a part of all patrols, unless, for whatever
reason, a team of only two is sent out, when for
cultural reasons, both must be men.

Internal gender issues

The CDTs also found that they had internal
gender-related problems to deal with, and a need
to address sexism in their own operations, and
between workers. As indicated above, women have
taken and continue to take positions of leadership
in the patrols. The gender imbalance in
recruitment is not easy to overcome, but is being
addressed through personal networking and
targetting of likely team members. There are
currently six women in the CDT team, including
the ‘reserve’ member, Anita Bogora. The question
of internal sexism must always, as in any
workplace, be addressed on an ongoing basis. The
CDTs have, however, built up a good relationship
of trust, and of using the briefing and debriefing
sessions to tackle issues together. (Women CD
workers have indicated that they do not want to
look at gender issues separately, but on a case-by-
case basis, as they came up in debriefing sessions.)
The increase in personal confidence and strength
reported by the women CD workers as a result of
their work also contributes to a resolution of such
issues; the final project evaluation reported that
“The female CDT members reported feeling less
intimidated and more able to assert themselves in
all situations.’!0°

4.4 Changes in work and
methodology

The praxis approach meant that the CDTs
were open to change their methodologies as
needed to fit the situation. Some of these changes
were simple. The CDT, for example, found that
even the use of the word ‘development in the
name of the team, was able to raise expectations of
‘cargo development’. They opted, therefore to
drop the word, and to refer to themselves while in
the field as ‘community workers’, and the team as

a community team. Again, the use of the 7ok Pisin
word evi (problem) was dropped, after the CDTs
realised that even asking this encouraged
communities to imagine that the CDT had come
to hear and then solve the problems. They
switched to asking what were the strengths and
weaknesses of the community, which had the
advantage of leaving the matter as a community
concern, and also of beginning with a positive, so
that communities avoided the taurangu trap, of
seeing themselves as purely pitiable, rather than as
being capable of planning their own future. !°

Another simple change was in materials used.
In the field the CDTs found there was some
awkwardness in using the venerable butchers-
paper method for recording group discussion,
since many of the members of communities visited
were not literate. They developed a series of
symbols using easily available materials such as
stones and leaves, placed on the ground during
discussion, to record agreed points. Later, as
appropriate, these symbols could be transferred to
the butchers-paper for use in follow-up meetings.

One small administrative change had an
interesting result, for whatever reason. It was
decided that women CDT members would handle
the money on CDT patrol, to pay for food and
other travel expenses; patrol expenses have
dropped 30% since this change was made.

Some changes were more fundamental to
project design. For example, it became clear in
practice that the step of committee formation,
which had been an expected outcome from Step
Four of the community development process, (see
Section 3.2 above) did not contribute to
community empowerment and self-reliance, but
instead had become the occasion for more
division. (See Box: The Pimbum Story II] —
Committee Formation.) As later analysed by CDT
member Grace Kua, in discussion of the
community development process across the AOI,
‘If one gets onto the committee, then the other
one says I want to be on there too. People start
fighting for power, not thinking about the
development of their community.’ It was therefore
decided to drop the committee formation step,

Impact on the project

The shock to the whole project was great, for
several reasons. Firstly, the CRI base had hosted
CDT training sessions since their inception, and
the conservation staff of the Bismarck-Ramu
project were permanently lodged there, and an
immediate concern was that there might be a
physical upheaval, if the project had to find a new
base. Secondly, the financial management of the
community development work — including the
payment of CDTs — had been done through
CRI, and new administrative systems had to be
found for these, as well as for the education work
done through CRI. Both these matters distracted
staff, and were a temporary, but real check to the
project: ‘The whole of CRI collapsing just took so
much of everyone's time, thinking of where we
could go, how we'd reorganize the administration.
That’s what I regretted more than anything, the
time we lost.’!!>

In the event, there was no physical dislocation,
and the base at the former CRI is still intact. The
financial management which had been done
through the CRI, including the employment of
the CDTs and of Leo Yat, was picked up by the
BCRMP. after protracted negotiations with the
senior administering agency, UNOPS.

A third impact was on the whole concept of
the project, the idea that the sustainability of the
project relied on the international NGO partner.
Very suddenly, the concept of self-reliance, the
cornerstone of the community development
process, shot home to the Bismarck-Ramu team.
This was an impact, but not an unpleasant one, as
the Bismarck-Ramu team realised that human
resources were available, both in government and
available by contract, capable of achieving the task
set.

But it was the fourth impact, the impact on
the education strategy, that was undoubtedly the
greatest blow for the project to come from the
disintegration of CRI. The former Director of the
CRI, who had been overseeing the Education
strategy, and planning out an environmental
education curriculum for the schools in the
Bismarck-Ramu, left the project, and then some
months later, left PNG. In this way, the project
lost its major education worker. The curriculum

work simply stopped short. School speaking work
had, for the entry phase only, been undertaken by
the CDTs, and later also by Leo Yat. Frequent
requests from schools in the area to speak, and to
give materials, requests cannot be ignored, in view
of the importance of the education strategy for the
design of the project. This work, therefore
continued, but increasingly, as their own work
expanded, it became clear that this needed
specialist attention, as well as the production of
appropriate materials for school use.

outcomes

At the end of December 1997, the
Madang-based staff of the Bismarck-Ramu project

Since the Project began 34 communities have been

Development process is at different stages in
approximately 22 of those communities. In all the
communities visited by the CDTs the members of
the teams have established good relationships with
the local people. Some communities are taking
self-initiative and are organising themselves to
address their identified problems.!?4

At Sepu, for example, after the entry phase,
and the empowerment through discussion, the
community entered the self-analysis stage of the
process. From this, they were able to identify
health and lack of education as their main
problems. Specifically, they wanted a pre-school to
boost literacy and the ability to benefit from later
education, they wanted an aidpost with a
Community Health Worker (CHW), and they
wanted to be sure that their water supply was fit
for drinking. (Doubts had been raised about the
water supply some years back — it will also be
remembered that 1997 was a year of drought, and
many usual water sources were much diminished).
With CDT help, they decided that they could
build a pre-school using local materials, and
identify at least two community members to train
as pre-school teachers. They could build an
aidpost, also from local materials, and restore the
house and garden formerly set aside for the CHW;
if this did not attract the Department of Health to
send them a health worker, they could decide
which of their own community should be sent for
training. They could send a sample of the available
water for analysis, and reconsider further action
accordingly.

By March 1998, the community had built the
house for the pre-school work, and had chosen six
community members for literacy training. It had
been decided that the language of instruction
would be Tok Pisin. By June training materials had
been delivered, and a training course organised, to
be led by CD worker Peter Moikia, who had
previous experience working in adult literacy. The
water had been analysed, and found fit for human
consumption; as a preventive health measure,
however, pig-pens had been constructed and the
pigs moved to the other side of the Ramu River.

A spin-off benefit from this was that with the pigs
safely behind fences, community gardens could
now be constructed closer to the village, thus
preserving more of the bush intact. The local
authorities of the Department of Health had
replied that they did not believe that the
population of Sepu warranted the appointment of
a CHW, and that Sepu people could travel to
Gariligut, several hours’ walk away, for their health
services. The Sepu population are still considering
what they want to do about this.

At Foroko-Brimde, there were certainly many
infrastructure problems. However, the worst
problem, from the point of view of community
development, was the unresolved question of the
place of the Jimi settlers in the Ramu bush area,
and the relationships between the settlers and the
landowners. The Jimi settlers had taken the lead in
meeting with the CDTs, and were ready to grasp
the message of self-reliance in development. They
had identified the lack of education as a major

knowledge of reading and writing before they

literacy, in order to read the bible. They therefore
decided, with CD worker encouragement, to

they had not yet equipped the health centre with

It is not always easy to see whether a question
is a community development question, or a matter
of managing the environment. At Gariligut, for
example, community troubles and anger at over-
exploitation, without proper authority, of swamp
resources and hunting grounds had led to the
‘owner’ of these grounds working ‘magic’ to make
them useless for food-gathering and hunting
purposes. When the CDTs talked about changes,
this change was one pinpointed by the community
as having been detrimental to the community,
since no-one could now benefit from the land,
including its ‘owner’. After discussion, and after
assurance to the community that they themselves
held the answer, the community agreed they did
indeed have the answer — the landowner would
work the counter-spell, and the swamp would be
fruitful once more. To the extent to which this
trouble was a breakdown of community cohesion,
springing from the anger of the ‘owner’ over others
exploiting the land, it was a community
development problem. To the extent that over-
exploitation had caused a temporary drop in the
biodiversity, it was a conservation problem. The
first solution which the ‘owner had found to the
problem, the first ‘magic’, simultaneously allowed
an explanation for the shortage of food on the
disputed lands, and also allowed the owner of the
use-rights of that land to assert his ownership as
well as power over the land. The solution was
partially effective, in that there was a release of
community tension — the owner had ‘worked off’
his anger, though the community was left to bear
the cost, in a diminution of food resources. The
first solution also, though this was not the avowed
intent, allowed the land a respite from hunting.
The second ‘magic’ allowed a further resolution of
the community dispute; the ‘owner’ having

asserted his claim to the land, could now ‘remove’
his ban, and allow once again the low-level
controlled exploitation which contributed to
community nutrition as well as harmony. The
following CDT patrol could testify to the
effectiveness of the solution they had helped to
bring about — they feasted on fish and crayfish, as
each meal became a celebration of the restored
community, and environmental, harmony.

Many other stories could be told from the
different communities visited. Not all, of course,

which underlies all of these steps. In sum,

expressed in many different ways. The most

literacy programmes and pre-schools. They have
changed village layout, for better hygiene, for

outcomes have grown directly from the
community development process

Strategy

The design of the Bismarck-Ramu project was
predicated on the belief that the building of
conservation conviction, including conviction
about the value of the environment for the
continued well-being of rural communities, was
essential for long-term biodiversity conservation.
To achieve conservation conviction, the project
used both the education strategy and the CD
process of analysis of change, self-reflection and
empowerment. The conservation strategy came
into play once communities had indicated their
interest in conservation to the CDTs, and
thereafter had demonstrated their commitment to
the concept by themselves seeking out the
Conservation Area Manager in Madang, getting
preliminary information from him, and inviting
him into their communities to ensure that the
whole community understood what avenues of
conservation were available.

Thereafter the Conservation Area Manager
was to become anchorman for the BCRMP in this
work, visiting communities and discussing with
them what conservation might mean, the different
options they could consider and so on. Through
all this, the ICAD staff remained aware of the
need not to rush the process. Just as in the
community development strategy, there were steps
in the conservation process. To attempt a hasty
legal reservation by hurrying through, or ignoring,
some essential stage would only lead to failure in
the end — the legal reservation might be an
accomplished fact, but it would not be reflected in
the actual use of the land.

7.1 Steps in the conservation
strategy

First communities must go through the
CD process, and from their own initiative express
an interest in setting aside some of their land for
conservation. The motivation is not pivotal, but
that the initiative comes from the community
itself and not from any outside agency is vital, in
the Bismarck-Ramu strategy.

Following from this, they must themselves
arrange to visit the DEC Conservation Area
Manager for the Bismarck-Ramu, paying for any
travel necessary. This is, in a sense, an earnest of
the depth of their interest, since travel in the rural
areas of PNG can be hard to arrange, and is
generally relatively expensive. Thus, the people of
Sepu had to hire a boat from Sepu, up the Ramu
to Brahmin Bridge, and then hire road transport
to Madang, in all a full day’s journey.

At this first meeting with the CAM, the
strategy is that he should begin talking with them
about why they want conservation, about different

practices, to open up the parameters of
‘conservation’ for later discussion. The CAM will
then settle with them a time for him to visit the
community, to discuss with them the possibilities

members in the community, to try to get the
widest possible attendance at that meeting.

The first conservation visit is to discuss with
the wider community just what is their motivation

might mean, including traditional ways of
conservation. Changes in PNG society are
discussed, and the community is asked to consider
how far their traditional ways will meet the

the fourth conservation visit.

The boundary along the river, though, is easily

get these this basic data. The community agreed

There is still a long way to go, in working
through the legal process of establishing a WMA.
The rules of management have to be hammered

most important step has been taken at both
Foroko/Brimde and Sepu: the community's
commitment to pursue formal conservation, not
in the expectation of ‘quick-fix’ compensatory
development rewards, but as part of their own
programme of development.

Lesson One: Site selection

The ‘lesson from Lak’ was that ‘socio-economic
criteria must dictate the actual choice of project
site. Before a commitment is made to a given area,
projects must collect information on local
institutions, community history ...’. 18? It was
suggested that ‘Social feasibility studies can be

was also stressed in the lesson from Lak that [CAD

Application

This lesson was influential in the first approaches

Bismarck-Ramu project design included a ‘social
feasibility study’, as outlined at Section 3.5 above.
As explained there, since this study was
undertaken by an expatriate researcher, it was
decided that his entry should in all cases follow the
entry of the CDTs. Therefore, it was not possible
to conduct the study, as recommended, ‘before a

However, the SFS has been used, and continues to
be used, in planning the conservation strategy

current politics and so on are also relevant.

This ‘lesson’ was that the style of the first entry to

for private and community gain. In terms of the

self-reliance, rather than looking to outsiders to

This lesson was integral to the Bismarck-Ramu

Self-reliance was a constant theme of all CD work

Reassessment

This lesson has proved in practice to be absolutely
accurate in analysis. The style and substance of the
community entry has been demonstrated to be of
great importance in determining the overall
success of the project, in avoiding the trap which
equates a ‘project’ with a patron-style delivery of
‘quick-fix development. However, it should be
noted that, as discussed at Lesson Fifteen below,
low-profile entry does not ensure that project
workers will not be approached with demands for
help of one sort of another.

Lesson Five: Co-operative
endeavour at the community
level.

This lesson claimed that ‘If ICAD projects are to
survive, they require that recipient stakeholder
communities already have a high degree of social
cohesion and co-operative endeavour’. The Lak
experience had been that ‘bigmen’ resented the
community-centred approach of the Lak ICAD, as
not giving them personally the patronage they
expected and as threatening their power. It was
suggested, therefore, that the social feasibility
study already recommended must include the
dynamics of contemporary community
interaction.

Application

The social feasibility analysis undertaken by the
Bismarck-Ramu ICAD, both the formal SFS and
the contributory work from the CDTs and from
John Opo, addressed this issue. However, the
CDTs did not insist on any exact level of
community cohesion before starting work in a
community, except the level required for the

community to agree as a whole that the CDT was

Reassessment

While the Race for the Rainforest lessons did not
specify the irreducible minimum ‘high level’ of
community cohesion necessary for project success,
the Bismarck-Ramu project showed that even
communities suffering division, to the extreme of
the Pimbum community, which had actually fallen
apart into smaller hamlets after fighting led to
blood-feuds, could recover community cohesion
sufficent to engage in the initiation of a
conservation ‘set-aside’. Long-term success cannot,
logically, be assessed within three years of ICAD
project initation, and a mere two years after the
revised project design. However, it can be said that
even communities which did not display strong
community cohesion were susceptible to the
steady community development work, generally
recognizing their internal divisions as a
community problem and moving to heal them, as
for example currently in Pimbum, or the efforts in
later 1997 and early 1998 of the Jimi settlers at
Foroko to find ways to achieve reconciliation with
traditional landowners. It should be noted,
though, that the disruptions which distracted
communities in the Upper Jimi to the extent that
CD work was temporarily halted (see Section 5.3
above) suggest that communities do need to have
stability and a minimum ability to focus on the
work at hand.

The Bismarck-Ramu project did benefit from
consideration of information about contemporary
community dynamics, and it would seem that this
should, as suggested, be part of any social
feasibility study.

this lesson that due to difficulties experienced by
the Lak team working with PNG Department of

national and international NGOs can bring to
project implementation many valuable skills and

They further suggested involving the NGO in

‘project philosophies, activities and outcomes.’

Application

This lesson was absorbed into the process of
rethinking the Bismarck-Ramu project beginning
in late 1995, and an NGO was involved in project
design, and contracted to deliver much of the
project management and activities. (The project is
also networking with other NGOs, and these may
become involved with the project if some liaison is
needed for implementation of community
development plans.)

In the event, those DEC officers most
involved in the project proved both committed
and efficient. In part this was due to the other
work of the BCRMP, in capacity-building inside
DEC; the new CTA spent much of his time in
1997 and 1998 in institutional strengthening.

The NGO, on the other hand, failed to
deliver. While there was undoubted enthusiasm

The ‘lesson from Lak’ concerning the limitations
of government departments remains valid, but it
must not be complemented by an unthinking
assumption that NGO skills or commitment will
be greater than government department skills or
commitment. Capacity-building must be part of
the overall project design, and time must be
allowed for this to have effect. The need for
diplomatic skills in working with bureaucracies

Lesson Seven: Communications
between stakeholders

The lesson drawn from the Lak experience was to
expect difficulties in communications between
proponents and local communities, arising from
poor infrastructure, remoteness, and language and
cultural barriers, and exacerbated by the
complexity of the issues to be discussed. The
authors warned that the difficulties could not
easily be overcome, but suggested using non-verbal
communication, using a local person as field
coordinator and using selected individuals as
‘controls — to assess the impact of the message on
them’.

Application
The Bismarck-Ramu project has taken cognizance
of this lesson, tackling the difficulty primarily by
taking a very patient approach, always checking
and rechecking to see that any position is
understood by all stakeholders. Long community
meetings are supplemented by sessions of
‘storying’ with community members.
Nevertheless, there were communications
difficulties. In some areas many community
members, especially women and the elderly, do
not speak Tok Pisin. The project workers had to
rely on translators, and these were not always
reliable. (See Section 4.3 above.) The project did
initially employ a local man as community liaison
officer, but found that this involved the project,
willy-nilly, in local politics. (1997 was a national
and provincial election year in PNG.)

Reassessment

The lesson in itself was borne out by the
Bismarck-Ramu experience; there are certainly
great language difficulties in working in rural and
remote PNG. It is suggested that at least part of
the difficulty can be overcome by allowing a long
time-frame for meetings and discussions; a period
of several days may be needed to discuss
something which in the absence of any language
difficulties might only take a few hours. The
Bismarck-Ramu experience of using a local person
as field coordinator was skewed by the exigencies
of an election year, and this suggestion cannot be
fairly judged from that experience.

Lesson Eight: Landowner
awareness

The ‘lesson from Lak’ following the experience of
running a Landowner Awareness Programme was
that ‘an extensive education programme was
required to lay the foundations’ of environmental
awareness. Such a programme would need to be
long-term and required the recruitment of

Application

The Education Strategy of the Bismarck-Ramu
project, which is explained at length in Part Five,
was developed precisely to meet this need, and two
staff members were recruited specifically for this
work. As well, aspects of the CD work contributed
to a growing environmental awareness, leading to
conservation conviction. For example, the
community mapping exercise described in

Part Four was primarily a part of CD work, acting
to begin community self-analysis, leading to
community planning. Part of the analysis, though,
was to draw the attention of communities to the
changes in their environment. With such
immediacy, communities could become aware of
how an environment can be depleted through
over-exploitation, before real devastation has taken
place, building conservation conviction while
there was the possibility of acting on it.

Two difficulties impeded the application of
this lesson. One was that initially, much of the
material produced by the education strategy, while
in itself of great value, was not useful within the
AOJ, because of low literacy rates; the final project

carried out... was not coordinated within the larger

PNG operation of an international NGO, which
had the secondary impact of the loss of the
education coordinator of the project, as explained
in Section 5.2 above.

Reassessment

The Bismarck-Ramu experience points to the
continuing validity of this lesson. In practice, an
intensive and long-term education campaign did

work to produce landowner awareness, and more
importantly, conviction that they themselves had
much to gain from the conservation of their land.

Lesson Nine: Staff on Strength

The conclusion drawn from the Lak ICAD
experience was that ICADs need ‘a relatively high
number of staff, particularly in the earlier years,’ to
cope with the complexities of the socio-political
situation, as well as the simple logistics of remote-
area operation.

Application

The Bismarck-Ramu ICAD benefitted from this
lesson. The full-time BCRMP staff at Madang
number only two, the DEC Conservation Area
Manager, and the UNDP Technical Adviser; in
addition the NGO was contracted to deliver the
services of the church liaison officer and the’
CDTs. (Following the closure of the NGO, the
church liaison officer and the CDTs have been
paid through the imprest account, as casual
labour.) The staffing level was effectively increased
by use of the consultancy budget; the education
coordinator and the CDT trainer were contracted
as consultants. There were also other consultants
employed during the term of the project,
including the SFS researcher, through a contract
with the National Research Institute, and other
trainers for the CDTs.

Reassessment

The lesson was reinforced by the Bismarck-Ramu
experience. The project could not have reached its
current level of achievement without the
employment of part-time and full-time workers on
local consultancy contracts.

Lesson Eleven: Establishing
a Site Presence

The Lak experience led those involved to the
conclusion that physical site presence should be
‘kept to the minimum level necessary for the
maintenance of stakeholder confidence’, since
investment in base construction can be a major
drain on project funds, and at Lak led to increased
expectations of rent payments among the
landowners. Nevertheless, the authors of Race for
the Rainforest concluded that ‘Logging companies
have a continuous presence... Conservation
proponents need to match this, and in this regard
a permanent presence is essential.’ The Lak project
deliberately constructed a high-value, durable base
as a sign of real commitment to the area.

Application

The Bismarck-Ramu project was in the fortunate
position that for a large part of the AOI, there was
no developer site presence, and no immediate
threat of resource extraction; the project was
therefore able to take a slow approach to base
establishment. The sign of commitment which the
Lak project offered through the base construction
was at Bismarck-Ramu tendered through the
repeated return visits of the CDTs.

In the event, the Bismarck-Ramu project
found that even the most minimal sign of a ‘base’,
such as the establishment of a radio at Pimbum in
early 1996, hampered project work, through the
raising of expectations, as at Lak. It was decided to
remove the radio, and to abandon any idea of
establishing a base in the AOI. This has meant
some added difficulty in communication with
teams in the AOI, and in arranging travel etc.
However, in the PNG situation, this was felt to be
a fair exchange for avoiding the unreasonable
expectations of rent and compensation.

Reassessment

The Bismarck-Ramu experience has not shown
that there is a need to rush into establishing a built
presence within the AOJ, even in those areas
where communities are involved economically

Lesson Thirteen: Maintaining
political neutrality

The Lak project found itself, despite care to avoid
any political affiliations, ‘being used as a political
football’, to score points for one or another local
political interest. They concluded that all potential
ICAD sites in PNG “are likely to contain a broad
range of political and economic factions’, and that
‘If projects intend to outlive the political life of
local power brokers, it is essential that politically,
they tread a middle line.’

Application

The Bismarck-Ramu project, also taking great care
to avoid political entanglement, also met this

difficulty, most strikingly when a consultant
employed by the project later became a candidate
in the national elections, but also in attempts to
draw project staff into such local political matters
as landowner-settler divisions. All workers used the
‘care and vigilance’ recommended by the authors
of Race for the Rainforest, and the project was
enabled to avoid being drawn into a seeming
alliance with any faction. However, it is recognised
that this is an ongoing problem.

Reassessment

The Bismarck-Ramu experience has demonstrated
again the importance of this lesson.

Lesson Fourteen: Operational
Needs

The ‘lesson’ here concerned the multiple
contingencies which can add difficulties to
remote-area projects, or indeed to any projects in
developing countries, and make it hard to meet
senior management expectations. The lesson was
rather directed to ‘the senior management of
parent agencies’ than to the project designers.

Application

It is hard to see how this lesson could be applied in
project design or operation, since it deals with the
extent to which operations will be hindered by
unexpected contingencies. This was certainly the
case for the Bismarck-Ramu project;
‘contingencies’ included a national political crisis
(the Sandline crisis), civil unrest at elections in
1997, severe drought and constant power and
telephone failure. The project was fortunate in
recruiting, for its final two years, a Chief Technical
Adviser who had had wide experience in
development work and diplomatic work, and was
adept at handling bureaucratic demands without
feeling them as a grievance.

Reassessment

The ‘lesson’ was once again borne out by the
Bismarck-Ramu experience.

Lesson Fifteen: Entering into
local social relations

The Lak experience was that entering into local
social relations entailed a complex and
insupportable load of ‘obligations’ which could
not be refused without incurring great offence,
and risk both to the project and to the personal
welfare of the staff. These ‘obligations’ related to
the perceived wealth of the project; villagers
demanded a share of that ‘wealth’, in the same way
as they demanded to share in the apparent wealth
of the resource extraction companies, in exchange
for allowing them access to resources. For the
companies, expecting great returns, these demands
are not difficult, as they are for projects on limited
budgets. Projects are not in a position to enter into
competition in this matter with resource
extraction companies and should offer only ‘a
progressive exchange of conservation for
development’.

Application

The Bismarck-Ramu tackled this matter in two
ways. The first was a policy of low-profile entry,
avoiding the display of wealth as far as possible.
Staff, including CDTs were frequently reminded
that they must be cautious about accepting any
gift, even gifts of food, since it m ight i involve the
project in reciprocal obligations.!#° The second
was that the CDTs did not ask for any concessions
or decisions from communities, but rather gave
help to them to work on their own community
development.

Even so, there were problems analogous to
those at Lak. At Dungo village, for example, ‘the
people said that 1.2 million Kina was allocated for
the implementation of the Bismarck-Ramu project
and so far they have not seen any benefit coming
from this money. !47 Other rumours went about
the AOI that the project had a budget of K2.5
million, prompting calls for this to be shared.
Some members of communities also criticised
CDTs for taking money for their work; ‘benefiting
out of it, making more profit, earning allowances
after patrols’. 148

Lesson Sixteen: Conservation
Initiatives are disadvantaged in
the present political climate

The Lak experience brought about the dispiriting
conclusion that ‘In the competition for Lak’s forest
resources, there seemed to be two separate sets of
“rules”, with one team playing fair, and the other
playing to win.’ There was no suggestion in the
‘lessons’ for how to overcome this, since it was
accepted that projects relying on public funds
must be clear, accountable and legal in their
disbursement.

Application

Since there was no suggested avenue to apply the
lesson, it cannot be said to have impacted on the
design of the Bismarck-Ramu project. However,
the project has tried to avoid the automatically
antagonistic approach to resource extraction
industries evident in Race for the Rainforest. This
was made easier by a site selection which allowed
an estimated two years’ work before entry of any
resource extraction industries into the AOI.
Further, the BCRMP management has tried to
pre-empt conflict by liaising closely with TNC

in its bid to undertake a sustainable logging
operation in areas adjoining the AOI, to act as an
alternative model of resource extraction.

Reassessment

While the Bismarck-Ramu experience has not
been the the baptism of fire that Lak proved to be,
the observation that private companies are not
bound by the tight operating rules which apply to
publicly-funded ICADs is still valid.

Lesson Seventeen: Participation

The Lak project, working against a desperately
short time-frame imposed by the imminence of
the logging operations, felt that there was no time
to involve the community in the ‘innovative and
ambitious solutions’ to the ICAD problem of
achieving economic development while conserving
the environmental resource. Those involved hoped
that a subsequent ‘substantial awareness and
marketing campaign [would] bring the local
community into the process.’ They had thus
hoped to work with full, if belated, community
participation, but found in the event that ‘several
stakeholders with legitimate rights to participate...
were actively working against the project.’ This
was a disappointment to the project staff. The
‘lesson’ drawn was tentative, suggesting ‘perhaps
the lesson is to maintain a position of
transparency, commitment and integrity, in the
hope that eventually other stakeholders will
recognise and appreciate this... perhaps the lesson
here is that the potential of local communities to
collaborate... should be a key factor in the decision
to locate a conservation project in any given area’.

72

Application

The Bismarck-Ramu project, since site selection
meant that there was not such a strong and
immediate threat to the environment, was able to
follow the path of working for and with full
community participation, without bringing any
pre-determined solutions to the economic
development problem. Instead, by stressing
community development, the community was able
to ‘own’ their own decisions on infrastructure and
economic development.

Reassessment

The Bismarck-Ramu project does not demonstrate
that there are insurmountable difficulties in
implementing the concept of community
participation. The abstract right of communities
to control their own development must take
precedence over project desire for ‘success’ in
project implementation.

Bismarck-Ramu story, then, is still unfolding.

stated in Chapter Two, above, the Bismarck-Ramu
project was set up, in part, to test out a working
hypothesis that, in the PNG context, genuine
community conviction about the need for

Marine WMA on Madang Coast

Another spin-off from the project is in CDT
member Peter Moikia’s village, Simbine, on the
north coast of Madang, in Bogia District. The
village lies on either side of a river, above a
whitesand beach, protected by ranks of coral reefs.

In this village, community development is
already well advanced, with success stories such as
a family trade store and poultry project. Now,
following the leadership and input provided by
Moikia, the community has decided to set aside
some of its coastal area as a Marine Wildlife
Management Area. The process began with clan
meetings of the Yakum Family Group, under the
Makakapur clan and of the Rarinkor clan. At these
meetings, clan members argued for conservation,
based partly on their history, and partly on
consideration of the future:

Throughout our cultural history, our tumbuna
history, we would usually look after one area and
find fish in other areas, so now we will use the
same system, for our children. Our children later
will see those things, the marine wildlife, still in
our area. It will be a no-fishing zone; we can get
adequate protein from non-protected areas.

This decision has followed the Conservation
Strategy outlined in Chapter Seven, above, with
the community having gone as far as agreement
among themselves, and the defining of land
boundaries, internal and external. A second clan
has asked to join this scheme, and now some seven
hundred metres of coastline, in the two separate
clan-holdings, extending one hundred metres out
to sea, enclosing the coral reef, has been designated
for the future Marine WMA.

Environment and Culture in Finschhafen

Another set of spin-offs from the project began

instituting a programme called simply
‘Thanksgiving’, where members contribute money

project was the rebuilding of the church. There are
also individual (and thus privately funded)
projects, which have been encouraged by the CD
process within the community. These include a
chicken farm, a piggery, tree planting, coffee
gardens, a fishpond and sheep-farming.

The CD work drew the attention of the

‘walkabout sawmill’, an easily transportable,
relatively low-impact logging operation.

In 1997 the Hakasobena Environmental and
Cultural Group decided to celebrate World
Environment Day in the village; this group
organised all the activities, and the day was a
success. But in 1998, the work had quite clearly

Many groups were involved. There were concerts,
with string bands, gospel singers and plays
depicting the first arrival of the first missionaries.
There were tumbuna singsings. There were

Skoro’s speech was very much in line with the

Other invited guests were there as well —

villagers have planted the vines which the

farming will begin in earnest. Planned for the

been set aside already. The Department of Primary

in principle, leaving the practicalities for a future
